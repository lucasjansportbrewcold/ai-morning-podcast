MIA: Morning, George. I have to start with the obvious one. The Riemann Hypothesis. Proved. With Claude.
GEORGE: Morning, Mia. Yes, the most famous open problem in mathematics, and the proof arrived as Lean code rather than as a paper. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, wrote, quote, "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." And he says the method extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.
MIA: And he didn't publish a human-readable version at all, right?
GEORGE: Deliberately not. He released it only as Lean code, in fourteen archive parts, because a "massive unreadable" human-language version would be, his word, "slop". Written out in full detail it would run past two hundred and fifty thousand pages. The Decoder describes it as seventeen million lines of Lean.
MIA: Which is wild, because the normal process is: you write it up, referees spend a year reading, and eventually the community believes it. Here nobody has read it.
GEORGE: And that's the interesting part. On the twenty-first of November the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, put out a joint statement: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof is correct."
MIA: Based on what, exactly? "Full confidence" from people who haven't understood it sounds odd.
GEORGE: Three layers. First, dozens of people compiled the library independently and reproduced the same proof export. Second, someone wrote a separate file that states the theorem, importing only Mathlib, and checked with tools called comparator and con-leche that it matches what Alpöge actually proved. That stops you proving something subtly different from what you claim. Third, the export was checked by Lean's own kernel plus several independent checkers written in different languages.
MIA: So it's a contract checked by four notaries who don't talk to each other, plus a separate person confirming the contract says what the title page says.
GEORGE: Exactly that. And Andrew Sutherland at MIT published a verification log: all one hundred seventy-five thousand one hundred eighty-seven modules compile with zero errors, the headline theorems depend only on three standard axioms, and a sandboxed rebuild with no network produced a byte-identical export of about sixty-one point six gigabytes. Those checks were run with Claude Code, incidentally.
MIA: How much did Claude actually do, versus Alpöge?
GEORGE: By his account, he steered the work toward the right analogue of a toy setting, and then "Claude far outpaced me near the finish line." His example is Claude suggesting that a chain of theta lifts should have length square root of X for a von Mangoldt sum of length X. He admits he has "little mastery of automorphic forms on higher rank groups", and that parts of his own timeline paraphrase a Claude summary that "might make zero sense".
MIA: Refreshingly honest. But I'll be the cynic: he's an Anthropic employee, the credit split is self-reported, and it landed right after the IPO.
GEORGE: He addresses that directly and calls the cynical reading "false". His timeline runs from the sixth of October to the sixteenth of November. But yes, the human-versus-Claude split is his own account. Two more caveats. The verified "GRH" covers Dirichlet L-functions only, not Dedekind zeta, Hecke or automorphic L-functions. And the certification rests on Mathlib's definitions being right and the kernels being sound. It says nothing about anyone understanding the proof. MIA: So AI referees the human proof, and Lean referees the AI proof.
GEORGE: Sutherland, quoted in Scientific American, called it "an historic moment" and "a human triumph", and said understanding the details "is going to take some time".

MIA: And what lit up?
GEORGE: Three hosting providers had been breached, the earliest in mid-July, and none of them knew until Dragos told them. GEORGE: Now, METR's forty-hour horizons make a nice setup for today's deep dive, because a new paper drops those same frontier models into a messy organisation, and the numbers look very different.
MIA: SWE-CoWork, from TokenWave AI, posted on the seventeenth. I've been waiting for someone to build this.
GEORGE: The idea is to stop handing coding agents a clean ticket. Each of the hundred and one tasks takes a real open-source repository and builds an organisation of twenty-six LLM-backed colleagues around it. Each has a role, limited local knowledge and rules. A colleague only discloses a requirement if the agent supplies enough code context. They can ask for a reproduction, refer you elsewhere, or say "later".
MIA: So, an actual workplace.
GEORGE: Very much. The agent gets a roughly two-hundred-word onboarding note, something like "finish the upgrade, the previous owner is on leave", and has to piece together requirements from board cards, versioned documents, chat, merge requests and CI logs. And the records are booby-trapped: superseded drafts, logs copied from the wrong run, cancelled cards, neighbouring projects.
MIA: How big is it?
GEORGE: Eleven languages, twelve domains, over ten thousand board cards, about forty-one thousand chat messages, and an eight-hour budget per run. Grading uses hidden tests: fail-to-pass tests for new behaviour, pass-to-pass tests for regressions. The clever bit is that every scored requirement is linked to an owner and to specific tests, so they can trace each one from the first question, to the code, to the handoff note.
MIA: Okay, the headline.
GEORGE: Opus 5.5 passes sixty-three point eight four percent of new-behaviour tests but completes only six point nine three percent of tasks. GPT-6 Astra: sixty-two point nine one and seven point nine two. No other model completes more than about one percent.
MIA: So almost two-thirds of the work, and roughly one task in fourteen actually done. From the same models METR puts at forty hours.
GEORGE: Different measures, but it's a sobering pairing. Regressions are fine, by the way: pass-to-pass sits around ninety percent for the top five. They don't break things. They just don't finish things.
MIA: What predicts the score?
