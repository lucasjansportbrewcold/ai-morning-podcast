## Deep dive: Who pays for the AI buildout? Van Nieuwerburgh's $10 trillion capital stack, the Hyperion template, and what a correction means for Dutch pensions

- Why this topic, why now:
  This week the Nasdaq 100 fell 10.4% and entered bear-market territory, Nvidia dropped 14% in one day on reports of an SEC review, and Anthropic fell 9.1% the day after its IPO [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/us-technology-stocks-suffer-their-worst-weekly-loss-since-the-pandemic-as-ai-doubts-pile-up/, citing the FT: https://www.ft.com/content/9f3c6ed6-c08f-4710-9683-ee2f5eb25841). "Is it a bubble?" is the wrong question for an economist. The better questions are how big the physical investment is, who has financed it, how much revenue it has to earn, and where the losses land if that revenue doesn't come. Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh (Columbia Business School) answers these in "Financing the AI Buildout", a paper for the Fall 2026 Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, now NBER w35865 (October 2026). His follow-up with Zijin Su, "Clouds of Credit" (NBER w36032), came out this month. The DNB published a Dutch scenario in ESB in October, and its broader AI study on 2 November. The Anthropic IPO and the Cowen debt-versus-equity reading were covered on 2026-11-19. This deep dive is about the infrastructure capital stack underneath, which has not been covered.
  - Source note: the full text used here is the BPEA conference draft dated 4 September 2026 (https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/4c_Van-Nieuwerburgh.pdf). The later NBER version (October 2026) changes the headline numbers in its abstract: a 188 GW buildout, "nearly $9 trillion", 3.2% of GDP per year. The draft has 183 GW, $10.3 trillion and 3.6%. Say both and use the draft for the mechanics. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w35865)

- Outline:
  1. **AI is a physical capital boom, and the arithmetic starts at the rack.** A 200 MW campus at a PUE of 1.2 gives 166.7 MW of IT load. At about 142 kW per Nvidia GB300 NVL72 rack, that is about 1,170 racks at $4.0m each, plus 20% for networking, storage and integration. The facility costs $11m per MW. Total: $8.2bn, about 68% IT equipment and 32% building and power. The economic centre of gravity is the part that wears out fastest.
  2. **From pipeline to scenario: $10.3 trillion, 3.63% of GDP.** Cleanview lists 2,933 live projects totalling 566 GW. The author does not take that pipeline at face value. He assigns each completion cohort a realisation rate: for example, 90% of 2026 projects with reported capacity are completed by 2032, but only 20% of undated projects. Missing capacities are imputed with a ridge-regularised regression. The result is 182.7 GW by 2032, 117.2 GW after 2032 and 226.9 GW never built. Costs are spread over the three years before completion. As a share of GDP, this is larger than any earlier US capex boom: canals, railroads, electrification, highways and telecom.
  3. **Why the money is moving off balance sheet.** For the five hyperscalers, capex exceeds operating cash flow for the first time in 2026. So financing shifts to leases, joint ventures, project debt, private credit, securitisation and SPVs. Frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic have no investment-grade credit of their own, so they get compute through hyperscaler contracts. In effect they borrow the hyperscalers' credit rating. At group level the equity/debt split looks like 60/40, but leverage at the asset level is much higher.
  4. **Case study: Meta's Hyperion and the "Beignet" vehicle.** A $30bn, 2 GW campus is about 90% debt-financed at a debt service coverage ratio of 1.12×. It rests on five four-year leases plus a residual value guarantee. Under GAAP, neither obligation is on Meta's balance sheet, even though one of the two will certainly materialise. Meta pays more than 100bp extra for this, over $5bn in extra interest. The point is not cheaper money. The point is a balance sheet that still looks asset-light. The same template has already been copied (Sopaipilla, Meta with BlackRock).
  5. **The revenue requirement: the number to remember.** Earning a 10% unlevered return at a 50% cash-flow margin requires about $3.7 trillion of annual revenue by 2032, 9.2% of 2032 GDP. That works out to $5.5 per installed GPU-hour, $6.9 at 80% utilisation. This is within the range of current rental prices. So the risk is not that today's price is too low. The risk is whether 180 GW of extra capacity can keep that price. The mechanism is the mismatch between six-year IT equipment and 20-year debt. If IT lasts three years instead of six, the requirement rises to about $6.0 trillion.
  6. **Where losses land, and the Dutch exposure.** The paper names four vulnerabilities: tenant concentration, obsolescence, execution, and circular financing (Nvidia guaranteeing its own customers' chip financing). The paper is explicit that it is "premature" to call this systemic risk. The follow-up paper (Clouds of Credit) estimates losses in a low-productivity state and the network propagation. For the Netherlands, the DNB sees the main vulnerability not in AI adoption but in pension funds' concentrated exposure to a handful of US tech firms.

- Facts and examples with sources:
  - The representative 200 MW campus costs about $8.216bn: facility $2.2bn (200 MW × $11m), incremental power infrastructure $0.4bn, IT equipment $5.616bn (1,170 racks × $4.8m installed; the bare GB300 NVL72 system is $4.0m per rack). IT is 68.4% of the cost. [primary] (https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/4c_Van-Nieuwerburgh.pdf)
  - Traditional CPU racks draw 5–10 kW. Current AI racks draw "well above 100 kW", and a GB300 NVL72 draws about 142 kW. A continuous 200 MW load uses about as much electricity per year as 170,000 average US households. [primary] (same URL)
  - Cleanview data (accessed 23 July 2026): 2,933 operational or planned projects after excluding 39 cancelled ones. About 57 GW is in operation, and the planned pipeline is about 509 GW. Including the existing stock, 566 GW is reported, against 39 GW at the end of 2024. Capacity was missing for 432 projects and was imputed (regression R² 0.846 in logs, ridge penalty chosen by cross-validation, Duan smearing correction). [primary] (same URL)
  - Central scenario: 182.7–182.8 GW operational in 2025–2032, 117.2 GW after 2032, 226.9 GW never completed. The 353.6 GW of undated projects is the biggest source of uncertainty: only 59.7 GW of it is assumed completed by 2032. The author calls this "a scenario rather than a forecast". [primary] (same URL)
  - Investment of $10.3 trillion over 2025–2032 ($41m per MW in 2025, +4% per year), an average of 3.63% of GDP. The annual series peaks in 2032 at $2,058bn, 5.1% of GDP. Historical comparison (average annual capex as % of GDP): canals 0.66 (1836–1841), railroads 2.24 (1870–1890), electrification 0.50 (1905–1925), highways 1.13 (1956–1973), telecom/fibre 1.10 (1996–2003). [primary] (same URL)
  - The author adds a caveat to his own historical comparison: GPUs can be economically obsolete within three to six years, while canals and fibre lasted decades. So each dollar of gross investment creates less net capital. On the other hand, the estimates leave out later hardware refreshes. [primary] (same URL)
  - The NBER version (October 2026) states in its abstract: "A 188 GW U.S. buildout completed by 2032 would require total investment of nearly $9 trillion and amount to annual investment of 3.2 percent of GDP over 2025–2032". The 1 GW cost of about $41bn is unchanged. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w35865)
  - Combined capex of Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Alphabet: about $97bn in 2020, more than $400bn in 2025 (chart: $415.8bn), projected above $800bn in 2026 (chart: $800.5bn). 2026 is the first year capex exceeds combined operating cash flow. The 2026 figures combine reported results, guidance and analyst estimates. [primary] (https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/4c_Van-Nieuwerburgh.pdf)
  - Morgan Stanley (cited in the paper): of about $2.9 trillion of incremental hyperscaler compute needs in 2025–2028, more than half comes from outside capital. The split is about 60/40 equity/debt. Within the debt, private credit is about $800bn, corporate debt about $200bn and structured finance about $150bn. [primary for what the paper reports] (same URL)
  - Hyperion: about 2.0 GW, about $30bn. Blue Owl bought an 80% stake for about $2.5bn, and Meta keeps 20%. The JV "Beignet" raised $27bn of debt in October 2025, rated A+ by S&P (one notch below Meta), described as the largest single investment-grade corporate bond issue in US history. Debt-to-asset ratio about 90%, DSCR 1.12×. Meta's own leverage at year-end 2025 was about 25% (book equity) or 4% (market equity). [primary] (same URL). In a Brookings podcast the author gives the DSCR as "around 1.15" [secondary summary] (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-10-trillion-question-financing-the-ai-buildout/). Use 1.12× from the paper.
  - The Hyperion leases are five four-year terms from 2029 to the bond's maturity in 2049. At each renewal Meta can terminate, after which the assets are sold and Meta pays any shortfall below a contractual minimum value (residual value guarantee). Under GAAP neither obligation is on the balance sheet before it takes effect. [primary] (https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/4c_Van-Nieuwerburgh.pdf)
  - Beignet's yield was 6.58%, at least 100bp above Meta's likely unsecured borrowing rate. That is more than $5bn of extra interest over the life of the bond. The paper's conclusion: "The economic rationale for Meta is therefore not cheaper financing, but preservation of an asset-light corporate profile." [primary] (same URL)
  - Moody's: hyperscalers have about $970bn of lease commitments, of which about $660bn is for future leases not yet on the balance sheet. A WSJ analysis cited in the paper counts $2.4 trillion off balance sheet for Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta ($904bn of future leases plus $1.52 trillion of purchase commitments, mostly chips), against $604bn on balance sheet. In that analysis, only about one fifth of the roughly $3 trillion of data-centre-related obligations is on the balance sheet. [primary for what the paper reports] (same URL)
  - Template copied: the 960 MW Sopaipilla project in El Paso (Meta with BlackRock) issued $12.3bn of A+-rated bonds in July 2026. Circularity: a $500bn credit facility from Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR (August 2026) to finance Nvidia chips, with a 25% residual value guarantee from Nvidia (up to $125bn of backstop). Google and Broadcom have a similar arrangement for TPUs. [primary for what the paper reports] (same URL)
  - The market beta of US data-centre REITs rose from about 0.5 to about 1 (36-month rolling window, monthly data January 2018 to March 2026). Data centres now behave less like a defensive infrastructure asset and more like part of the same systematic risk. [primary] (same URL)
  - Revenue requirement (Appendix D): the 182.7 GW costs about $8.54 trillion, or $9.61 trillion including the return on capital during construction. At a 10% unlevered return, with a six-year life for the 68% IT share and 20 years for the rest, the annual capital charge is 19.37%. That requires $1.86 trillion of operating cash flow, which at a 50% margin is about $3.725 trillion of revenue by 2032 (9.2% of 2032 GDP). Sensitivity range: $2.29 trillion (5% return, 67% margin) to $6.72 trillion (15% return, 33% margin). [primary] (same URL)
  - In market units: about 77 million GPUs (1.07 million racks), so $5.5 per installed GPU-hour at full utilisation, $6.9 at 80% and $7.9 at 70%. The paper puts market prices for high-end Nvidia capacity at roughly $6 to over $10 per GPU-hour on demand. With a three-year IT life, the requirement rises to about $6.0 trillion (14.8% of GDP), or $8.8 per GPU-hour. [primary] (same URL)
  - Context in the paper: with combined revenue of OpenAI and Anthropic of about $100bn, revenue would have to grow about 80% per year to 2032 to reach the $3.725 trillion. [primary] (same URL)
  - The author's conclusion: "The relevant question is therefore not whether AI infrastructure is already systemically risky, but under what conditions project-level losses could become correlated and propagate." Policy recommendation: better measurement and disclosure of the whole capital stack (project debt, JV stakes, future leases, residual value guarantees, tenant concentration). The goal is transparency, not banning off-balance-sheet financing. [primary] (same URL)
  - In the Brookings podcast (8 October 2026) the author says about $1.4 trillion of debt has been issued on these structures, roughly half held by banks. He sees "a real parallel" with pre-2008 structured investment vehicles in off-balance-sheet risk, but points to two differences: less short-term funding, and investment-grade tenants. [secondary] (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-10-trillion-question-financing-the-ai-buildout/)
  - Clouds of Credit (Van Nieuwerburgh and Su, NBER w36032, November 2026), abstract: equity cushions protect creditors against the obligations outstanding in mid-2026, but "concentrated exposures and interconnected claims create weak links". Equilibrium investment exceeds the social optimum because firms do not internalise their effect on competitors' revenues. Debt finances nearly 60% of the expansion. In the low-productivity state losses reach $16.8 trillion across shareholders and creditors, with 40% loss-given-default on debt. Suppressing contractual payment spillovers between the key firms reduces private losses by about 30%. [primary, abstract] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36032)
  - DNB scenario in ESB (De Vette, Serrano Ascandoni and Hoeberichts, 9 October 2026): the equity risk premium rises from about 4.4% to its 2002–2025 average of 6.8% (+240bp), run through NiGEM. Dutch equities fall just over 30%. GDP is 0.8% below baseline in year one (about 1% in the article's summary box), unemployment +0.2 percentage points, business investment about 3% below baseline and almost 5% a year later. The ECB cuts 50bp in year one. After three years GDP is 0.4 percentage points below baseline. [primary] (https://esb.nu/correctie-ai-aandelen-raakt-ook-nederlandse-economie-en-pensioenfondsen/)
  - Dutch exposure (same ESB article): pension funds hold about €270bn in US equities and fund participations, and about €160bn in tech companies, of which about €110bn is US. That is roughly 45% of their listed equity portfolio and about 9% of total assets. Households have about €19bn of direct exposure to the US private sector. The authors' conclusion: the main Dutch vulnerability is concentrated financial risk in a few tech firms, not direct dependence on AI. [primary] (same URL)

Adoption is uneven: large firms and digitally intensive sectors lead, while small firms and construction, transport and hospitality lag. One recommendation is to keep reliable, affordable access to compute and avoid vendor lock-in. [primary] (https://www.dnb.nl/en/general-news/news-2026/ai-offers-opportunities-for-the-netherlands-but-success-is-not-a-foregone-conclusion/)

- Skeptic angle (for the script):
  - Everything rests on one scenario. The realisation rates (20% for undated projects, and so on) are assumptions, not estimates, and the undated category dominates the pipeline. The author himself cut the headline from $10.3 trillion to "nearly $9 trillion" between the September draft and the October NBER version.
  - Costs are measured per megawatt, not per unit of compute. If efficiency improves sharply, capex per MW stays the same, but the revenue per MW that the market will pay can fall. In the podcast the author answers this with Jevons (cheaper compute means more demand). That is a hypothesis, not evidence.
  - The revenue requirement assumes a 50% cash-flow margin across the whole chain. Who captures that margin (the chip maker, the hyperscaler, the model lab, or the end user) is not modelled in this paper.
  - "Premature to call this systemic risk": the data on private credit and syndicated loans, the bulk of the financing, is not public. The paper says this itself. So the most important exposure is also the least visible.
  - The DNB scenario holds expected profits constant and lets only the risk premium move. A correction driven by disappointing AI revenue (lower profits) would be a different, potentially larger shock.

- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week:
  1. **Rebuild Appendix D as a small model** (spreadsheet or Python): capital base × [0.68·a(r,6) + 0.32·a(r,20)] / margin. Then vary IT life (3, 4, 6 years), utilisation and margin. Within an hour you get a better intuition than any bubble op-ed, and a reusable tool for each new capex announcement.
  2. **Agent project on the measurement gap the paper names:** an extraction pipeline over 10-K and 10-Q filings that pulls out future lease commitments, purchase commitments and residual value guarantees, with source citations per number and a human acceptor. This is exactly the work Moody's, the WSJ and Atrium.ai are now doing by hand or commercially. It is a typical case for document data extraction with verifiable output. It could also be relevant for Dutch pension-fund analysts or the DNB itself.
  3. **For his own stack:** the $5.5–$8.8 per GPU-hour range is the floor that infrastructure owners need to earn back. Model API prices that sit structurally below it are being subsidised by somebody. Keep model choice portable (the DNB's vendor lock-in point) and route on cost per solved task.
