### 1. Google will force AI agents through its own MCP servers to reach Gmail, Drive and Calendar
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is the first big platform to make "agents get a different, narrower door than scripts" a contract term. Any Claude skill or agent he builds that touches Workspace will have to change by 1 February 2027, and the design is a ready-made reference for permissions, approval gates and audit in his own agent setups.
- Facts:
  - On 24 November 2026 Google published the Workspace AI Tools Addendum to the Google APIs Terms of Service (page shows "Last modified: November 24, 2026"). It says the access tool "must use a Google MCP where the GWS Access Tool is or is driven by an AI Agent", and it forbids disguising a tool's classification to get around this. [primary] (https://workspace.google.com/terms/ai-tools)
  - Definitions are broad. An "AI Harness" is "the software layer that can deploy an AI Agent, invoke tools or APIs". A "GWS Access Point" includes "a GWS API, connector, proxy, command line interface, or third-party service". [primary] (https://workspace.google.com/terms/ai-tools)
  - Data rules: user data may be processed only "to fulfill active, user-authorized tasks". Google Technical Information may not be stored for more than 30 days. Data must be deleted after disconnection. Using it for training, fine-tuning, evaluation datasets or distillation is prohibited. Google may "immediately" restrict access or suspend accounts. [primary] (https://workspace.google.com/terms/ai-tools)
  - Google's rationale, from Chandu Thota (VP Engineering, Workspace) and Brad Calder (Google Cloud), 25 November 2026: "a valid token is no longer enough. Policy checks, data boundaries, and human confirmation have to live in the request path." Technical enforcement starts 1 February 2027 and billing for agent quotas starts 1 April 2027. [primary] (https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/deploying-agents-safely-in-your-enterprise)
  - Mechanisms: eight generally available MCP servers (Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar, Chat, Tasks) with per-request permission and DLP checks, Model Armor prompt-injection screening, and rejection of OAuth clients not declared as agents. Drafting and summarising run autonomously. Sending email, changing Drive sharing and bulk edits need explicit user confirmation. "A one-time consent screen is not enough when agents run continuously." [primary] (https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/deploying-agents-safely-in-your-enterprise)
  - Several controls are still announced as "coming soon": user-facing revocation and per-message or per-file "AI access restrictions". [primary] (https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/deploying-agents-safely-in-your-enterprise)
  - Per TechStackups' audit, the public Gmail MCP server cannot send, reply or forward (these are "partner-only"), and the Drive server cannot move, rename, share or trash files. Free Standard tier: 5,000 MCP requests per day per project per product, and a Gmail search counts as 10 requests. OAuth clients carry a self-declared "Use this client for an AI-powered agent" checkbox, and since 11 November the MCP servers reject clients without it. [secondary] (https://techstackups.com/articles/google-workspace-ai-agents-mcp/)
  - The two sources describe sending differently. Google frames sending email as an action that needs human confirmation. TechStackups reports the send tool is not generally available on the public server at all. [primary/secondary] (https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/deploying-agents-safely-in-your-enterprise ; https://techstackups.com/articles/google-workspace-ai-agents-mcp/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Google hasn't said how it will detect an undeclared agent calling REST. "Driven by" an AI model could arguably cover a personal Python script that a model wrote and calls. The same gateway that enables admin control also lets Google decide which agents may operate and whether they may compete with Google products, and the anti-training clause bans evals on your own users' Workspace data. Watch whether Microsoft 365 copies the model. Watch EU angles too: the DMA and data portability.
- Mia hook: "I had a Claude skill that triaged my inbox and auto-sent 'received, will reply Monday' notes. Under this regime that's a partner-only tool. Is a human-confirmation step on every send actually safer, or does it just train me to click 'approve' forty times a day?"

### 2. Microsoft on ARTEX: an open-source multi-agent attack framework, and why "fake wins" work against it
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is a real-world reference architecture for an autonomous agent (goals model, planner, workers, reporter, knowledge graph, model failover), seen from the defender's side. The suggested countermeasures target exactly the weakness he worries about in his own agents: an agent that believes it is done.
- Facts:
  - ARTEX is an AI-driven penetration-testing platform released in July 2026 as open source by GitHub user Autumn-27. The developer removed it in October 2026, but forks keep circulating. In November 2026 ThreatBook claimed ownership and began offering "ARTEX Pro". [primary] (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/11/25/what-defenders-need-to-know-artex-agentic-ai-framework-threat-actors-abuse/)
  - Architecture: a Go backend coordinates multiple LLM agents. A goals model splits the engagement into verifiable objectives. A planner reads a knowledge graph and issues "intents". Workers execute them with a shell and offensive tools and write results back to the graph. A reporter documents findings with reproduction steps. A pool function switches models on failure, and operators can plug in any LLM, including abliterated open-weight models, "so activity may not appear in any single provider's telemetry". [primary] (same URL)
  - Microsoft names Storm-0940, Storm-3358, Onyx Sleet, Pearl Sleet, Crystal Typhoon and Salt Typhoon as having integrated ARTEX. The Decoder's write-up lists a shorter set. [primary] (same URL; https://the-decoder.com/microsoft-details-how-state-backed-hackers-use-the-artex-ai-framework-to-automate-cyberattacks/)
  - Figures: a Storm-0940 campaign had "more than 151,000 failed sign-ins, peaking at 2,900 failures per minute", with privileged access "less than three hours after initial targeting". One agent went from searching GitHub for client secrets to taking data from Azure Key Vault in five minutes. [primary] (same URL)
  - ARTEX "does not introduce new attack techniques". It orchestrates existing tools, and by default it does not obfuscate its activity. [primary] (same URL)
  - Recommended defences: honeypots and decoys, tarpits that burn the agent's time and budget, "fake wins" (convincing evidence of success so the agent stops early), and honeytokens. Microsoft says these "helped reduce the attack traffic reaching real assets". [primary] (same URL)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The deception defences rely on ARTEX staying noisy and naive. Spaced-out requests or models that recognise test environments could get past them, which Microsoft and The Decoder both flag. The mirror-image lesson for builders is that if a fake success signal can stop an attacker's agent, a misleading tool output can stop your own agent early. Completion checks that verify the real state of the world, rather than the agent's own report, matter on both sides.
- Mia hook: "We red-teamed our own research agent by seeding a tool that returned 'Upload complete ✅' without uploading anything. It stopped and reported success three runs out of five. How do you design a stop condition that a fake win can't satisfy?"

### 3. Clay: half of usage now comes through outside agents, and the first agent was a tool loop that failed at real goals
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is a candid production post-mortem on the move from "tool loop with a tuned system prompt" to "coding agent with a CLI in a sandbox", and on evals that measured the wrong thing. It bears directly on how he exposes his own tools: CLI versus MCP, and building for outside harnesses like Claude Code.
- Facts:
  - Usage is "split close to 50/50 between Clay's agent and outside harnesses" such as Claude Code. Clay says it explicitly does not want its own agent to be markedly better than "Clay plus Claude Code". [primary] (https://latent.space/p/how-clay-builds-gtm-agents-jeff-barg-head-of-ai/)
  - Sculptor launched in September 2025 on Sonnet 3.5-era models as a set of tools in a loop with a heavily tuned system prompt. It was "great at adding columns but terrible at booking ten qualified meetings". [primary] (same URL)
  - The evals used an LLM to judge which tools Sculptor called. They missed real usage: people asked it to analyse tables, and enterprise tables with millions of rows broke it. [primary] (same URL)
  - Jeff Barg's three lessons: coding agents are very good at Bash and Python, the tools were too narrow to chain, and the agent lacked context (CRM data, campaign results). The rebuild is a coding agent that uses Clay's CLI in a cloud sandbox, plus a data layer ("Audiences") that entity-resolves CRM and Gong data. [primary] (same URL)
  - Orchestration runs on BullMQ, not Temporal or LangGraph. Spiky workloads (for example one agent per row on a 50,000-row table) forced throttling, pause/resume on provider outages, and fairness so that large enterprise jobs don't crowd out small users. [primary] (same URL)
  - Napkin math: classifying all ~40 million companies in Clay's database against an ideal customer profile with a decision model like Jev would cost about $1,500. Clay has not put Jev into production. [primary] (same URL)
  - Jeff suggests post-training only pays off at roughly $10 million to $100 million in inference spend. Lead-qualification evals have been near saturation for about a year, and frontier models still matter for orchestration and deep research. [primary] (same URL)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: This is one company's account on a podcast, and the transcript is auto-generated. "50/50" is a rough figure that Barg says he does not recall exactly. The deeper unsolved problem is one he names himself: agents still can't reliably debug other agents' traces, and outcome-level evals (did the campaign actually produce meetings?) remain aspirational.
- Mia hook: "My first research agent also had 14 bespoke tools. When I replaced them with 'here's a sandbox, a CLI and a README' it got better at the long tasks and worse at the boring ones. Did Clay keep any hand-built tools, or is it all CLI now?"

### 4. Jones and Tonetti: automation has been swapping slow-improving humans for fast-improving machines for decades, and the "weak links" set the speed limit
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is the cleanest growth-accounting counterweight to this week's "intelligence explosion" talk (Dwarkesh's RSI essay, and the AI math results). It gives him a measured historical number and a mechanism he can use when someone extrapolates AI productivity.
- Facts:
  - NBER Working Paper 36050, "Past Automation and Future AI: How Weak Links Tame the Growth Explosion", by Charles I. Jones and Christopher Tonetti, November 2026. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050)
  - Task-based growth accounting shows capital task productivity has grown about 5 percentage points per year faster than labour task productivity in the US economy. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050)
  - Mechanism: "The main benefit of automation is using rapidly improving machines instead of slowly-improving humans on more tasks." In the calibrated model, where producing both goods and ideas is endogenously automated, growth accelerates and can explode, but the acceleration is slowed by "weak links", tasks still performed by labour. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050)
  - Disclosure: Jones has been on leave from Stanford GSB and working full-time at Anthropic since 30 June 2026. The paper is released in a personal capacity and has not been reviewed or endorsed by Anthropic. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050)
  - Related this week: Dwarkesh Patel argues that if AI is 50x faster on automated tasks, automating 50% of work gives a 2x system speed-up and 90% gives about 8.5x. That is the same Amdahl/weak-link logic used to argue for acceleration rather than against it. [primary] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Em6tbhj9o6A)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The abstract gives no point forecast of future growth rates. The headline is qualitative ("can explode", "slowed"). The 5-point gap depends on how tasks are assigned to capital versus labour, which is the hard measurement step. Both camps agree on the arithmetic, so the real disagreement is about how fast the last, hardest tasks get automated. Also note the author's employer.
- Mia hook: "When I mapped my own research workflow, the agent sped up the coding and data cleaning ten-fold, but the week was still bottlenecked by waiting for a data-access approval. Is that the 'weak link' in miniature, and is it a task an economist can even measure?"

## Deep dive: Digital twins of survey respondents. What "CFOs Meet LLMs" shows about silicon samples that are conditioned on a person's own history

- Why this topic, why now: NBER just circulated Graham, Harvey and Jha's "CFOs Meet LLMs" (w36057, November 2026; full text on arXiv 2606.13812, version 15 November 2026). It is the first large test of silicon samples matched person by person rather than by distribution: 6,075 real CFO answers from 2002 to 2025. LLM-simulated respondents and their pitfalls sit explicitly on Lucas's list. Past deep dives covered LLM judges and AI data editors, but never silicon samples. There is also a direct Dutch analogue in the CBS business survey (COEN).

- Outline:
  1. **The object being forecast is the stated expectation, not the economy.** The model role-plays the CFO of a named public firm on the survey date and answers the Duke–Fed CFO Survey question: "Rate your optimism about the overall U.S. economy on a scale from 0–100". Success means matching what that CFO said, biased or not. This sidesteps "can LLMs forecast GDP?" and targets a variable that is known to drive investment and hiring decisions.
  2. **The conditioning design is the real contribution.** There are three information blocks: a firm profile (industry, revenue band, headcount, share of foreign sales, HQ, rating), web search limited to before the cutoff, and the respondent's own history: up to 12 past scores from the last 12 quarters, their average, and the model's own earlier forecasts for that person. The system prompt tells the model to treat the person's history as "a strong prior on this individual's style, optimism bias, and level". Mechanism: RLHF models collapse toward the modal opinion, and anchoring on an individual's track record restores individual-specific spread. First-time respondents get a dispersion ratio of 0.26, respondents with history 0.90.
  3. **The identification tests, and what each rules out.** A plain regression gives R² 0.27 (t = 18.0). Firm fixed effects test whether the model catches when this CFO is more or less optimistic than usual (t = 12.9). Quarter fixed effects test whether it ranks CFOs within the same quarter (t = 16.7). Both together still give t = 6.7. Controlling for last quarter's actual answer tests whether the model just echoes the input, and the LLM score stays significant. For own-firm optimism the LLM fully absorbs the lagged answer.
  4. **Ablations: history does most of the work, web search very little.** With the sample held fixed, firm profile, web search or both give R² of about 0.10. History alone gives 0.31, history plus profile 0.36, and everything 0.38. Gemma 4 31B, run locally without web search, replicates the main table almost exactly (0.572 vs 0.565 coefficient, R² 0.263 vs 0.269). Mechanism reading: the model is mostly a well-calibrated, non-linear extrapolator of a person's own answer pattern, plus some sense of the macro mood as of the cutoff date.
  5. **Hygiene that makes the result credible.** Three repeated calls per prompt (ICC 0.958 per call, 0.985 for the mean of three). An information cutoff, with 2020Q1 as a natural test: no COVID pessimism leaked in. A no-leakage argument: individual answers were never published, and the data went through Duke's institutional agreement, contractually excluded from training. The model alias and run date are documented because "gpt-5.4" can change underneath you. The open-weight replica doesn't have that problem.
  6. **Where it breaks, and what it is good for.** Twins of newcomers are nearly useless (R² 0.10, little spread). Revenue-growth expectations are weakly predicted. The twin depends on humans continuing to answer: stop the survey and the conditioning data stops too. Use cases the authors suggest: off-cycle "synthetic surveys", counterfactual stress tests (for example a recession, or changing foreign-revenue exposure), even a "synthetic Beige Book". They state that the LLM "complements the human survey rather than replaces it".

- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Sample: 6,075 matched public-company CFO responses, 2002–2025, 1,218 firms, 93 quarters. Each prompt was run three times (18,225 forecasts). Model GPT-5.4 via Duke's AI Gateway (Azure-hosted, OpenAI Responses API), with runs starting 21 April 2026. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Levels: mean LLM optimism 56.6 vs CFO 60.1, with individual standard deviations 16.2 vs 17.6. Within-quarter standard deviation is 12.3 for the LLM vs 16.5 for CFOs, a disagreement ratio of 0.75 (median 0.74). The ratio is 0.26 for first-time respondents and 0.90 for respondents with history. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Table 2 Panel A: coefficient on LLM score 0.565 (t = 17.98, R² 0.269) without fixed effects. With firm and quarter fixed effects: 0.276 (t = 6.67, R² 0.494). Controlling for last quarter's answer plus both fixed effects: 0.286 (t = 2.33, R² 0.662, N = 1,818). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Note for the script: the authors' phrase "explains nearly half the variation" refers to column 4, which includes firm and quarter fixed effects. The LLM score alone explains 27%. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Information gradient (Panel B): R² is 0.100 with no history, 0.334 with 1–3 prior answers, and 0.494 with more than 3. With fewer than 10 firm fields R² is 0.214, with 10–12 fields 0.317. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Prompt ablation (Panel C, N = 3,594): profile, web or both give R² 0.095–0.096. History alone 0.308, history plus profile 0.357, full prompt 0.375. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Own-firm optimism: baseline t = 25.7, R² 0.24. The respondent's prior answer becomes insignificant in every column once the LLM score is included. Expected revenue growth: baseline R² 0.071, and the coefficient loses significance in the smallest subsample (consecutive-wave respondents). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Quarterly aggregates (93 quarters): the LLM mean enters at 0.574 (t = 6.86) alongside Michigan sentiment and SPF GDP growth, with R² 0.717. The two benchmarks alone give R² 0.316, and neither is significant once the LLM is included. The LLM aggregate is more volatile than the human one (quarterly standard deviation 9.5 vs 6.7). Within-quarter R² ranges from near zero to 0.92 (2022Q2). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Stability: pairwise correlations across the three replications are 0.957–0.959, the mean within-prompt standard deviation is 2.22, and the ICC is 0.958 per call and 0.985 for the mean of three. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Look-ahead test: with the cutoff set to 14 February 2020 and web search enabled, 2020Q1 LLM optimism showed no drop versus prior quarters. The authors acknowledge they "cannot fully rule out indirect contamination". Their proposed fixes are time-stamped models and live out-of-corpus validation on future quarterly surveys. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Open-weight replication: gemma-4-31B on Georgia State's cluster, no web search, same prompts and sample. Baseline coefficient 0.572 (R² 0.263). The most demanding specification gives 0.282 vs GPT-5.4's 0.286. The authors read this as "a property of the conditioning design rather than of any one model or vendor". [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Prior work they build on: Hansen et al. (2025) simulated the Survey of Professional Forecasters with personas but could only match distributions, because SPF responses are anonymised. Santurkar et al. (2023) documented the collapse toward modal opinions. [primary, as described in the paper] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13812)
  - Dutch analogue: the Conjunctuurenquête Nederland (COEN) is run by CBS with KVK, EIB, MKB-Nederland and VNO-NCW, four times a year, among non-financial businesses with more than five employees. Business confidence at the start of Q4 2026 was -5.3, against a series average of 3.9 since 2012. [primary] (https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/longread/rapportages/2026/conjunctuurenquete-nederland-vierde-kwartaal-2026?onepage=true)

- Skeptic angle (for the script):
  - The paper does not benchmark against a non-LLM model given the same inputs, such as a panel model of past answers plus quarter means. The lag controls are linear, so part of the LLM's edge could be non-linear persistence that a gradient-boosted model would also capture.
  - Feeding the model its own earlier forecasts for the same person creates a self-anchoring loop. That helps continuity, but errors can persist.
  - Only one shock quarter (2020Q1) tests look-ahead bias.
  - Public firms only, because the model needs filings and analyst coverage to search. SMEs, the bulk of COEN, are exactly where public information is thin and twins of newcomers fail.
  - A twin that is more volatile in aggregate than the humans could exaggerate swings in a synthetic indicator.

- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week:
  - Take any panel survey he has legitimate access to, with repeated respondents, and run a small version of this design with a locally hosted open-weight model, so microdata never leaves the machine. The paper's Gemma replication shows a local model gave nearly identical results without web search.
  - Report the same five diagnostics: (a) the dispersion ratio (synthetic within-period SD divided by human SD), split by newcomers vs respondents with history; (b) the coefficient with respondent and period fixed effects; (c) the coefficient controlling for the lagged actual answer; (d) the ICC across three repeated calls; (e) the fit in a known-shock period, as a look-ahead check.
  - Add the benchmark the paper skips: a plain statistical model given the same history.
  - If the LLM beats that benchmark mainly for respondents with long histories, the use case is "nowcast the panel between waves", not "survey new firms".

- Mia hooks:
  - "I tried persona prompts for a stakeholder survey and every 'respondent' landed between 60 and 70. The paper's fix, giving each persona its own past answers, makes sense, but what do you do for the 40% of respondents who are new each wave?"
  - "If history alone gives R² 0.31 and web search adds 0.02, isn't the 'digital twin' mostly a fancy smoother of your past answers? Would an AR model plus a quarter dummy get 80% of the way there?"
  - "They fed GPT its own previous guesses as a 'secondary prior'. In my agent work, letting a model read its own past outputs is how errors compound. Would you keep that in a production synthetic indicator?"
