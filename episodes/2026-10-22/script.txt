MIA: And I'll say up front, I built exactly that. An internal knowledge base: router, embeddings, top-k, a two-line summary. People kept asking for things that weren't in there and blamed the model. It turned out nobody knew what was in there.
GEORGE: Well, this paper puts numbers on that. Rakesh Allu at Illinois, Gies, and Evgeny Kagan at Johns Hopkins, Carey. Pre-registered on AsPredicted, posted to arXiv in August, and announced in the general economics section this week.
MIA: Walk me through the setup.
GEORGE: Three hundred sixty-two participants from New York and New Jersey, recruited through CloudResearch Connect, randomized into three arms. Filter, a classic faceted search, a hundred and four people. Chat, a hundred fifty-six. Hybrid, filters plus chat, a hundred and two. Each had to pick the event they'd most like to attend from a scraped Ticketmaster New York City catalog of events with tickets under two hundred dollars. About four thousand events per wave: four thousand sixty-six in March, three thousand eight hundred thirty-six in April. Seven hundred twenty-five distinct event names, a hundred thirty-six venues, forty-two genres.
MIA: And the outcome isn't "how satisfied are you", which is what makes me take it seriously.
GEORGE: Right, it's incentive-compatible. After choosing, people state a willingness to pay with the Becker-DeGroot-Marschak method, on a multiple price list from zero to fifty dollars. And one in twenty participants actually received the ticket or the cash.
MIA: For anyone who hasn't run one: with that mechanism the best strategy is to state your true value, because a random price decides whether you buy, and your stated number only decides whether you accept at that price. Lying can only hurt you.
GEORGE: And the clever methodological bit is that the chat is pure RAG over the same catalog. So for a given query, every arm returns the same events. Any difference comes from the interface, not from the model making things up. They checked this with a thousand Monte Carlo queries per wave.
MIA: That's a neat trick. You pin the LLM's outputs down by tying them to retrieval. I wonder whether it would work for something like a silicon-sample study, though, where the whole point is the model's own judgment, not a list from a database.
GEORGE: I don't think it would. The trick works precisely because the answer space is a closed catalog. Once you want the model's opinion, you've lost the anchor.
MIA: Okay, the pipeline. Because I want to know if the chat arm is a straw man.
GEORGE: It isn't. It's roughly what most of us would build in a weekend. Each catalog row is serialized as key-value text and embedded with the ada zero zero two embedding model into a FAISS index. A router LLM, GPT five point two at temperature zero point one, rewrites the conversation into a single search query, or asks a clarifying question if the request is vague. MIA: And the generation step?
GEORGE: Temperature zero point two. It writes a one or two sentence reply plus a tagged list of up to a hundred event IDs. Any ID it invents is dropped by intersecting with the catalog. Results are ranked by similarity to the query.

GEORGE: And they validated retrieval. On structured test queries, the chat found the target event in ninety-six point nine and ninety-eight point five percent of cases across the two waves, and had it in the top ten about ninety-five percent of the time. Filter and Hybrid, a hundred percent.

MIA: So facets aren't just a UI convenience. They're information. They show you the schema of the world you're searching in.

MIA: And the real-world pattern the authors point to fits their story. GEORGE: Right. So, design fixes. I count four. First, keep the structure visible: a hybrid UI, or render the facets inside the chat itself.
MIA: Second is the one I like most: self-querying. The LLM translates the request into a structured query, metadata filters drawn from declared fields plus a semantic search string, and the vector store executes it. LangChain's docs describe this for stores like Chroma, Qdrant and MongoDB Atlas.

MIA: Third, route by intent. Chat for known-item lookups, facets and browsing for exploration. The router can make that call, as long as it doesn't answer vague queries with an interrogation.
