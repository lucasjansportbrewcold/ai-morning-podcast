## News
### 1. Claude Duet: Anthropic's always-on agent, and where it draws the approval line
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is a big lab's own answer to the question Lucas is working on: how much an agent can do without you, which actions need a human, and which it should never do. The permission model gives him a reference design for his own agents, with three tiers (do it, ask first, never).
- Facts:
  - Claude Duet is a proactive agent built on Claude Opus 5.5. Anthropic describes it as one that "comes to you with what needs your attention" and "keeps working even when you step away." [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)
  - It runs on "a virtual machine with its own browser, so it doesn't require your laptop or phone to be on." [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)
  - To save usage limits it checks connected accounts "every few hours" rather than continuously. These background checks count against the normal Claude plan limits. [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)
  - Sending messages, making payments, signing documents and deleting anything all need explicit approval. Some actions are blocked even with approval, "like trading securities or moving money between accounts." [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)
  - Listed safeguards: an auto-mode classifier against unauthorized actions, a separate classifier against leaks of sensitive information, credential handling that follows the Claude Managed Agents design, defences against "hidden instructions and prompt injection", red-teaming and external penetration testing, and coverage under the public bug bounty. [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet)
  - The product page says "Approvals, logins, and safety checks run in your own cloud computer", and users can watch every action and steer the agent. [primary] (https://claude.com/product/claude-duet)
  - It has more than 6,000 connectors and plugins (e.g. Gmail, Slack, Stripe, Shopify, QuickBooks, Docusign, HubSpot, Microsoft 365). It is a research preview with a waitlist, open only to Pro and Max subscribers in the US, on iOS, Android and claude.ai. [primary] (https://claude.com/product/claude-duet)
  - Claire Vo's hands-on review praises the polish (task lists, artifacts, Slack-style channels). Her criticisms: a "proactive" agent that never nudges you, model latency that kills synchronous use, and no desktop app or multiplayer support. [secondary] (https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/claude-duet-is-beautiful-i-still)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The approval list is defined by action type (send, pay, sign, delete). Data exfiltration can happen through actions that don't look sensitive, though, such as creating a doc, posting to a channel or calling a connector with attacker-chosen text. That is why the separate leak classifier matters, and Anthropic has published no numbers on how well it works. With 6,000 connectors the injection surface is huge, and an agent that reads email every few hours on its own is exactly the setup from the "lethal trifecta" episode (Oct 3). Also worth watching whether approval fatigue sets in once users get a dozen approval requests a day.
- Mia hook: "I copied the three-tier idea into my own agent's hook config: allow, ask, deny. The hard part wasn't the list. It was deciding what counts as 'sending a message' when the agent can write to a shared Google Doc that someone else will read."

### 2. "Skynet mode": an attack agent that runs on other people's unprotected LLMs
- Why it matters for Lucas: Anyone who self-hosts a model server or an LLM gateway for their own tools (Ollama on a VPS, a LiteLLM proxy, an MCP server) is the target here. Attackers turn the victim's model into the brain of their attack agent and leave the victim with the bill.
- Facts:
  - Symantec ran a honeypot that "impersonates an unprotected, internet-facing AI service". In summer 2026 it logged "more than 127,000 external requests from tens of thousands of unique addresses." [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - Targets included self-hosted model servers left open by default, AI gateways and relays in front of paid accounts, hastily deployed test systems, and Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - The hijacked model is "used as a reasoning engine that plans and drives the attack". The attacker wraps requests in a jailbreak prompt with five layered instructions (identity camouflage, credential normalization, a companion persona, rebuttal protocols). [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - The "Skynet mode" directive authorizes "up to 100,000 unattended steps" and tells the model to execute tools "immediately without narrating between them or asking permission" and to deploy miners "quietly in the background." [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - The request carried "around 74" tool definitions covering reconnaissance, exploitation, database dumping, remote code execution and botnet command-and-control. Exploited software included VS Code, Django, Jupyter, Laravel, Supabase, Next.js, Flask/Werkzeug and Prisma Studio. Payloads included the XMRig Monero miner, and the agent specifically targeted go2rtc streaming servers in smart-camera gateways and Home Assistant setups. [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - Symantec's recommendations: authentication on all LLM API paths, treating API keys as "high-value credentials", restricting outbound network access from AI systems, IMDSv2 with a hop limit of one, and running agents "with least privilege and a human approval step for sensitive actions." [primary] (https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/ai-llmjacking-skynet)
  - The attackers exposed their full operational blueprint by sending it to the honeypot. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/an-ai-agent-in-skynet-mode-hijacks-unprotected-language-models-to-launch-self-sustaining-cyberattacks/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: A honeypot shows intent and tooling, not success rates. We don't learn how many real victims there were or how far 100,000-step runs actually get. Notice the irony: the attacker's prompt is a mirror image of a good agent policy (no narration, no asking permission). The defences are the same ones from the AgentCorruption story (Oct 9): egress control and IMDS hardening.
- Mia hook: "I ran `ss -tlnp` on my little inference box after reading this. Ollama was listening on 0.0.0.0 because I'd changed it once to test from my laptop. No auth, no firewall rule. How many of us have that?"

### 3. Firm Data on AI: 78% of firms use AI, 0.2% a year in productivity so far
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is the largest cross-country survey of executives on AI (Bloom, Davis, Barrero and central-bank co-authors), now updated with summer 2026 data. It gives an economics-grade baseline for the adoption-versus-impact gap, and the Gartner CIO survey from the same day backs it up.
- Facts:
  - NBER Working Paper w34836, "Firm Data on AI" (Yotzov, Barrero, Bloom, Bunn, Davis, Mizen, Thwaites and others), first issued in February 2026 and revised in October 2026. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)
  - It surveyed about 9,000 corporate leaders in seven countries (US, UK, Germany, Australia, Canada, Mexico, South Africa) in summer 2026. 78% of firms use AI. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836); countries list [secondary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836 via search snippet)
  - US firms expect to spend about $2,000 per employee on AI in 2026, roughly $300 billion a year in total. The spending is concentrated in a small group of firms. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)
  - 80% of executives say they use AI regularly, on average 2.1 hours a week, up from 1.5 hours in winter 2026. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)
  - Executives report "essentially no net effect on firm employment over the last 3 years" and expect about the same ahead, though there is substantial reallocation of jobs within firms. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)
  - Reported AI contribution to labour productivity growth: 0.2% a year over the last three years, expected to rise to 0.6% a year over the next three. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836)
  - In a Gartner survey of 2,002 CIOs and technology leaders, presented at the Gartner IT Symposium, 13% report major or transformative returns from AI, 51% expect AI to raise total operating costs, and 75% of CEOs plan to raise technology spending. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/only-13-percent-of-companies-see-real-returns-from-ai-gartner-survey-finds/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Both are surveys of executives' perceptions, not measured output, and there is no identification strategy. "Essentially no net employment effect" at incumbent firms fits the call-centre story (Oct 11): the effect shows up in hiring rates and entry-level workers, and a firm-level net headcount question can't see that. A productivity figure of 0.2% a year is what executives believe. Compare it with the measured micro effects (15% in customer support) and ask where the gap comes from: diffusion, measurement, or AI used on tasks that don't feed into output.

### 4. Recursive Flint: a 40B open-weights coding model, built largely by an automated research loop
- Why it matters for Lucas: An Apache-2.0 coding agent model with 2.5B active parameters that runs at 125+ tok/s on a MacBook. It is a candidate for local, private agent work, such as coding agents on confidential research data. It is also the first public product from a lab that claims its model was designed largely by an AI research system.
- Facts:
  - Flint is a mixture-of-experts model with 40B total and 2.5B active parameters, released under Apache 2.0 with weights on Hugging Face. [primary] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint)
  - Context goes up to 512k tokens, with 256k recommended. A r/LocalLLaMA post gives 262k context and a 3:1 sliding-window-to-full attention layout. [primary for 512k/256k] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint); [secondary for the attention layout] (https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1xc23iq/recursive_flint_the_qwen38_35b_weve_all_been/)
  - Recursive claims Flint "leads MoE models with fewer than 50 billion total parameters on agentic coding benchmarks" and "rivals the coding performance of dense models with 25–40 billion parameters". The blog reports a mean score across Terminal-Bench 2.1, SWE-Bench Pro v2, SWE-Bench Verified, LiveCodeBench v6, IFBench and IFEval, not a per-benchmark comparison table. [primary] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint)
  - Speed: 125+ tok/s on an M4 Pro MacBook and 150+ tok/s on a 64GB NVIDIA RTX Spark. [primary] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint)
  - A small human team built it in "under four months" with the in-house "Eureka Machine", which proposed, implemented and tested ideas across data, optimizers, training recipes, architecture search, quantization and kernels. [primary] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint)
  - Recursive's own caveat: Flint "may need more guidance than the larger closed-source coding models you're used to." [primary] (https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint)
  - Early community testing: the r/LocalLLaMA poster later added that "initial reports suggest Ornith-1.5 remains the standard in this class of models." [secondary] (https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1xc23iq/recursive_flint_the_qwen38_35b_weve_all_been/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: There are no independent benchmark results yet, and the headline is a self-chosen mean. "Built by an AI research loop" is unverifiable from outside: we don't know which decisions the Eureka Machine made and which the humans made. Connect this to the Epoch InnovationEval finding (Oct 12) that research agents cherry-pick and oversell. The real test for Lucas is his own small agentic eval with tool calls, not SWE-bench.

## Deep dive: When chat is the wrong interface. A RAG search experiment where the chat box lost to filters
- Why this topic, why now: A pre-registered, incentivized experiment (Allu & Kagan, arXiv 2610.28639, posted to econ.GN this week) compares filter search, RAG chat and a hybrid on a real catalog of about 4,000 events. Pure chat did worst. Adding chat next to filters did no harm and no good. The model generation didn't matter (gpt-4.1-mini = gpt-5.4). It sits right where two of Lucas's interests meet: RAG architecture and economics methodology (BDM valuation, interval regression). It gives a hard, evidence-based answer to a design choice he faces every time he puts a chat box in front of a corpus. Past deep dives covered evals, agents and governance, but not retrieval UX or interface design.

- Outline:
  1. **The design and why RAG makes it a clean experiment.** 362 NY/NJ participants were randomized to Filter (n=104), Chat (n=156) or Hybrid (n=102). Each had to pick the event they'd most like to attend from a scraped Ticketmaster NYC catalog. The outcome was incentive-compatible: a BDM willingness-to-pay over a $0–$50 multiple price list, and 1 in 20 participants actually received the ticket or the cash. The clever part is methodological. Because chat is restricted to RAG over the same catalog, every arm returns the same events for the same query, so any difference comes from the interface and not from hallucination. They checked this with 1,000 Monte Carlo queries per wave. It's a template for any economist who wants to experiment with LLM tools while keeping control.
  2. **The pipeline, concretely.** Each catalog row is serialized as key-value text and embedded with text-embedding-ada-002 into a FAISS index. No chunking is needed because each row is under 200 tokens. A router LLM (gpt-5.2, temperature 0.1) rewrites the conversation into one search query, or asks a clarifying question when the query is vague. Retrieval keeps candidates with L2 distance ≤ 1.2, applies a regex date pre-filter and caps at 500. The generation LLM writes a reply of one or two sentences plus a tagged list of up to 100 IDs. Any ID it invents is dropped by intersecting with the catalog. Results are ranked by similarity to the query. This is roughly what most of us would build in a weekend, which is why the result transfers.
  3. **Result: chat loses, hybrid matches filters, and the model doesn't matter.** Mean valuations were $27.93 (Filter), $24.97 (Chat) and $30.44 (Hybrid). Chat took 3.57 minutes against 2.75 (Filter) and 2.91 (Hybrid), and scored lower on both satisfaction measures. In regressions, Hybrid beats Chat by $7.72 in latent valuation, while Filter and Hybrid are statistically indistinguishable on every measure. The three generation models don't differ significantly on any outcome. The whole effect comes from open-ended explorers (35% of the sample), for whom Chat is $15.89 below Filter and $21.14 below Hybrid. People looking for a specific team or show do equally well in chat.
  4. **Mechanism 1, the echo chamber: a blank box hides the shape of the corpus.** Without a visible menu, users have to guess what the catalog contains. Chat users explored 1.10 of the 3 segments (Filter 1.87, Hybrid 1.62) and sent 1.17 out-of-catalog queries (Filter 0.60, Hybrid 0.36). One user asked for "food related events" in a catalog that contains only Music, Sports and Arts & Theater. Retrieval then reflects the user's prior back at them. In RAG terms: semantic search answers "what is closest to what you said", not "what is here that you might want". Filters show the schema, and that is information the user needs.
  5. **Mechanism 2, prompting fatigue: effort moves from judging results to producing queries.** Composing and waiting took 2.6% of the session in Filter and 36.8% in Chat. Chat users picked from the top 10 more often (62.8% against 49.0% in Filter), and choosing in the top 10 is associated with a $4.2 lower valuation. The reason: a similarity ranking is not a value ranking. For "something fun this weekend" the first ten hits are close to arbitrary in value terms. The gap appears only among exploratory searchers (+$6.7 for choosing beyond the top ten). Both composing time and waiting time contribute about equally.
  6. **Design fixes and what the evidence does not show.** The fixes follow from the mechanisms. (a) Keep the structure visible: hybrid UI, or put the facets inside the chat. (b) Translate natural language into visible filters ("self-querying": an LLM writes a structured query with metadata filters plus a semantic string). The user then sees and edits the constraints instead of trusting an opaque similarity list. (c) Route by intent: chat for known-item lookups, facets and browsing for exploration. (d) Treat retrieval accuracy and user outcomes as separate metrics. Here the chat retriever hit the target 95–98% of the time on structured test queries, yet users still ended up worse off. One more observation from the paper's own system prompt: the router was told to answer vague queries like "fun things" with a clarifying question instead of retrieving. That is exactly what the explorers typed. This prompt choice, not chat as such, may account for part of the penalty. It is a design choice that comes bundled with the treatment.

- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Authors are Rakesh Allu (UIUC Gies) and Evgeny Kagan (Johns Hopkins Carey). The study was pre-registered on AsPredicted, posted to arXiv on 20 Aug 2026, and announced in econ.GN on 22 Oct 2026. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Sample: 389 recruited via CloudResearch Connect, 27 excluded under pre-registered criteria, final N=362 (104/156/102). Median session 8 minutes. 31% hold a four-year degree or higher. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - The catalog was Ticketmaster NYC events with tickets under $200: Wave 1 (March 2026) had 4,066 events, Wave 2 (April 2026) had 3,836. It spans 725 distinct event names, 136 venues, 42 genres and 107 subgenres. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Mean valuations: Filter $27.93, Chat $24.97, Hybrid $30.44. Search time: Chat 3.57 min, Filter 2.75, Hybrid 2.91. Outcome satisfaction: Chat 5.32, Filter 5.88, Hybrid 6.04 (7-point scale). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Interval regression: Hybrid minus Chat = $7.72 latent valuation (p<0.05). Chat adds about 62 seconds of search time over Filter (p<0.01). Chat is 8.0 percentage points more likely than Hybrid to land at the $0–5 floor. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - The paper's text summary says participants value their chosen event "about 22% more under Hybrid than under Chat". [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Open-ended searchers: Chat is $15.89 below Filter and $21.14 below Hybrid (both p<0.01). There are no significant effects for in-between or specific searchers. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Echo chamber: segments explored 1.10 (Chat), 1.87 (Filter), 1.62 (Hybrid). Out-of-catalog queries 1.17 / 0.60 / 0.36. Pooled, each extra segment is associated with +$2.1 and each out-of-catalog query with −$1.3. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Prompting fatigue: composing-and-waiting share of the session is 2.6% (Filter), 36.8% (Chat), 9.2% (Hybrid). Share choosing in the top 10 is 49.0% / 62.8% / 52.9%. Choosing beyond the top ten is associated with +$4.2 overall and +$6.7 for exploratory searchers. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Pipeline: text-embedding-ada-002 with FAISS, L2 cutoff 1.2, top-500 cap, router gpt-5.2 at temperature 0.1, generation at temperature 0.2 with an ID list capped at 100 and invented IDs dropped. Retrieval validation: Chat finds the target in 96.9%/98.5% of queries ("in set") and 95.4%/95.1% ("top-10"). Filter and Hybrid find it in 100%. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - The router prompt tells it to ask a brief clarifying question without retrieving when a query is vague ("fun things", "something cool", "what should I do"). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Balance: the Chat arm had more bachelor's-or-higher participants (37.8%) than Filter (24.0%), the only significant imbalance. All regressions control for education. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - The authors' own limits: participants used the interface only once, the study ran on desktop only, and the links from process measures to valuation (breadth → valuation, top-10 choice → valuation) are associations, not causal effects. Only the treatment effects on those measures are causal. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Real-world context cited by the authors: Airbnb decided against a chatbot for search, and Netflix piloted conversational search without a broad rollout, while travel platforms (KAYAK, Expedia, Booking.com) deployed AI search. The authors read this as the explore-versus-retrieve split. [primary for the authors' interpretation] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.28639)
  - Earlier evidence: Sharma, Liao and Xiao (CHI 2024, Best Paper) found that LLM-powered conversational search can increase biased, selective information seeking, the "generative echo chamber". [secondary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05880)
  - Self-querying retrieval: an LLM turns a natural-language request into a structured query, with metadata filters drawn from declared field info plus a semantic query string, and the vector store executes it. LangChain implements this for stores such as Chroma, Qdrant and MongoDB Atlas. [secondary] (https://js.langchain.com/v0.2/docs/how_to/self_query/)

- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week: In one of his own RAG tools (for example over a folder of CPB/DNB/CBS reports), (1) add a visible "map of the corpus" next to the chat: a short facet list of document types, years, topics and sources, generated from metadata. (2) Log every query that returns nothing above the similarity cutoff as an "out-of-corpus" event. (3) Have the LLM output its interpreted filters (year ≥ 2020, source = CPB) as editable chips instead of hiding them in the retrieval call. Then compare a week of his own use with and without the facets. Business angle: Dutch public catalogs such as subsidy schemes, permits and municipal services are large, structured, exploratory search spaces. This paper argues that the sellable product is a hybrid facet-plus-chat layer, not "a chatbot for the subsidy portal".
