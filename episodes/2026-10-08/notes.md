### 1. Claude Haiku 5.5: a tenth of the price, but a hungrier tokenizer and a 100k cliff
- Why it matters for Lucas: the cheap tier is the one he would put in loops that run unattended (feed triage, extraction, classification, sub-agents). The pricing has a cliff at 100k tokens and a tokenizer change, so the real saving depends on his prompt lengths, and long-context RAG calls may now be cheaper on a different model.
- Facts:
  - Released 7 October 2026; API model name `claude-haiku-5-5`; available on the Claude Platform, AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)
  - Pricing per 1M tokens for prompts up to 100k tokens: $0.10 input, $0.50 output, $0.01 cache reads, $0.125 cache writes. Above 100k tokens: $0.50 input, $2.50 output, $0.05 cache reads, $0.625 cache writes. Haiku 4.5 was $1 input / $5 output. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)
  - Anthropic says the new tokenizer, shared with Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5, uses slightly more tokens per task. Simon Willison measured the same long prompt at about 1.25x as many tokens on Haiku 5.5 as on Haiku 4.5, which he calls a hidden price increase. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5) (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/claude-haiku-5-5/)
  - Willison: up to 100k tokens Haiku 5.5 costs the same as OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna ($0.10/$0.50). Luna's own price step comes at 272k tokens and only goes to $0.20/$0.75, so above 100k tokens Luna is "a much better deal". [primary] (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/claude-haiku-5-5/)
  - Benchmarks reported by Anthropic for Haiku 5.5: OSWorld 2.1 72.4% (offline subset), GDPval-AA v2.1 1620 Elo, Humanity's Last Exam 45.9% without tools and 57.4% with tools, Terminal-Bench 4.0 39.2%, FrontierCode 1.1 46.4%. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)
  - The Decoder reports the Haiku 4.5 comparison figures: OSWorld 15.7%, Terminal-Bench 4.0 0.0%, HLE with tools 18.7%, GDPval-AA 735. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/claude-haiku-5-5-arrives-with-massive-price-cuts-proving-the-ai-pricing-arms-race-is-far-from-over/)
  - Reasoning cannot be turned off; it defaults to medium, with low, medium, high, xhigh and max levels. Willison's low-effort pelican SVG cost 0.0936 cents and took 7 seconds; the max-effort one took 5 minutes 9 seconds and cost 3.3826 cents. [primary] (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/claude-haiku-5-5/)
  - Same day: Sonnet 5.5 cache-read price halved to $0.10 per 1M tokens, which Anthropic says cuts agentic task costs by about 20%. Max 5x subscribers get $100/month of API credit, Max 20x $200/month, Team up to $500/month. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)
  - Willison adds that the credits don't roll over and need an API organisation linked under Settings > Billing. [primary] (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/claude-haiku-5-5/)
  - Anthropic says Haiku 5.5's cyber safeguards are stricter than Haiku 4.5's but looser than Sonnet 5.5's. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Vendor benchmark tables don't tell you what a task costs per completed item. Forced reasoning plus the tokenizer can wipe out the headline cut on short classification calls. The 100k cliff penalises exactly the long-context RAG calls a cheap model is tempting for. Willison notes the max-effort reasoning trace mentions the pelican test by name, which is a reminder that public "vibe" benchmarks leak into training.
- Mia hook: "I swapped Haiku 4.5 for 5.5 in my triage step and the bill only fell by about half, not 90%. Was that the tokenizer, the forced medium reasoning, or a few prompts over 100k? How do you even measure cost per task rather than cost per token?"

### 2. OpenAI publishes 722 math manuscripts from an unreleased model, and mathematicians push back
- Why it matters for Lucas: this is AI research output at a scale no human field can referee, which is the same problem he will face with agent-written replications and extensions in economics. The release format (paper + formal proof + reasoning summary, with a stated "may have issues" caveat) is a template worth comparing to what an economics version would need.
- Facts:
  - The `openai/math` GitHub repo holds 722 manuscripts in 372 families, produced by an unreleased internal OpenAI model, with preprints, a Lean formalization library and reasoning summaries for selected results. [primary] (https://github.com/openai/math)
  - About 4,000 research problems were posed during the evaluation; on average each result used three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking compute. [primary] (https://github.com/openai/math)
  - The repo says not all results have Lean formalizations and that "some of the unformalized results could have issues". Most results come from the same procedure, with documented exceptions such as the work on the Riemann zeta function and the Hodge Conjecture. [primary] (https://github.com/openai/math)
  - Reasoning summaries cover 10 results, including the irrationality exponent of π, the Mahler conjectures and the Mézard–Parisi formula for spin glasses. [primary] (https://github.com/openai/math)
  - OpenAI consulted the Institute for Advanced Study's Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI on how to release the results. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/openai-dumps-372-ai-generated-math-proofs-on-github-telling-the-academic-world-to-keep-up/)
  - Latent Space reports commentator claims, such as a "Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis" result and integer multiplication faster than n log n, and itself notes these "have not been independently verified". [secondary] (https://www.latent.space/p/ainews-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-openai)
  - Context: on 11 September 2026, 25 Fields Medal winners, including Terence Tao, Peter Scholze and Maryna Viazovska, signed "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics". It argues that solving problems is a proxy for conceptual understanding and that rushed AI announcements harm the field. It does not call for banning AI. [secondary] (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/25-winners-of-maths-nobel-prize-decry-the-ai-invasion-of-their-discipline/)
  - A Hacker News commenter who worked on Barnette's Conjecture on and off for 24 years described seeing it listed as solved (problem 180) and feeling sad "in a far-off way". [primary] (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/jake-boggan/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Lean verifies that a proof is valid, not that the formal statement is the right one or that the result matters. The unformalized part is explicitly unchecked. "Three hours per result" averages over successes, and the 4,000-problem denominator matters for the hit rate. Counts like "90 of the top 500 problems" come from aggregator headlines, not from OpenAI. Watch which results survive community checking over the next weeks, and whether journals accept any of it.
- Mia hook: "If an agent dumped 700 replication extensions of Dutch economics papers on GitHub tomorrow, who would check them? Is there an econ equivalent of Lean: a deterministic re-run of the code that forms the 'verified' tier, with everything else labelled 'may have issues'?"

### 3. Anthropic's Cyber Verification Program: autonomy granted by identity tier, not by prompt
- Why it matters for Lucas: it is a production example of the idea he keeps returning to, that what an agent may do should follow from verified context and a permission table, not from what the prompt says. It also shows the trade-off: lifted blocks come with mandatory data retention.
- Facts:
  - Announced 6 October 2026, with three tiers. Defense Access covers SOC work, incident response, malware reverse-engineering and vulnerability analysis; it is open to company security teams, nonprofits, universities, government, critical infrastructure, open-source maintainers and individual researchers with a disclosure record; review takes "a few days". [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - Red Team Access adds authorized penetration testing and red-teaming, for organisations only, with review in "a few weeks". Real-time blocks remain on physical harm, mass disruption, ransomware and high-risk safety-system testing. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - Specialized Access has the fewest cyber blocks, is limited to verified organisations testing safety-critical systems (flight systems, power grids, telecoms, interbank infrastructure, government networks), and needs US government collaboration for approval. [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - Covered models: Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Mythos 5.1 and future models. Generally available models keep "conservative cyber safeguards that block most cyber work". [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - Project Glasswing partners found 129,000 verified vulnerabilities from April to July 2026, more than 33,000 of them critical or high severity, and Anthropic estimates the real impact is "at least five times higher". [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - On Anthropic's CyScenarioBench, Defense Access blocked 46 of 50 tasks; Red Team Access blocked none and completed 34 of 50 (the page reports a 67.6% success rate). [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
  - Data retention is required for misuse monitoring; a zero-retention option (Enterprise Frontier Safeguards) is due "later this fall". [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Vulnerability counts come from partners and Anthropic, with no independent audit, and the "five times higher" figure is an estimate. Identity verification moves the risk to account takeover and insider misuse. The top tier depends on US government vetting, which raises questions for EU organisations. Glasswing members move to the top tier automatically, without reapproval.
- Mia hook: "This is basically RBAC for model behaviour. Could I do the same for my own agents: one model, but the tool set and blocklist chosen from a verified session tier in code, never from the conversation?"

### 4. AI study podcasts work for weaker students, if a named human verifies them first
- Why it matters for Lucas: it is a clean economics DiD on AI-generated podcasts plus FAQs, essentially Morning Signal's format in a classroom. It also shows something he can reuse as a methods point: the mean effect is fragile while the threshold effect is robust, so report distributions.
- Facts:
  - Authors: Canh Thien Dang and An Nguyen (King's Business School, King's College London). Setting: compulsory first-year Principles of Economics at a UK business school; two cohorts of 85 students each, 340 exam marks. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07097)
  - Intervention, 2024/25: for the macro half (Part 2), the lecturer used Google NotebookLM on the slides, readings and assessment guidance to generate a ten-minute podcast, FAQs and a study guide per week. A named graduate teaching assistant checked every output against the sources and regenerated where needed. Materials were labelled "AI-generated and academically verified". Part 1 and the previous cohort got nothing comparable. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
  - Difference-in-differences: +2.34 marks on a 50-mark component (p = 0.045 HC1; 0.013 student-clustered). This reflects protection from a decline: Part 2 rose 0.73 (not significant) while Part 1 fell 1.61. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
  - The share below the upper-second boundary (30/50) fell 24.7 percentage points relative to the counterfactual (p = 0.001). Effects are significant at every threshold from 23 to 31 marks and none above. About three-quarters of the average comes from the bottom quintile; the 10th-percentile gain is +11.0 marks. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
  - Robustness: dropping the 5 lowest pre-period students cuts the mean effect to 1.56 (not significant), while the below-30 effect stays between −19.9 and −22.9 pp with p ≤ 0.006. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
  - Cost: about 30 minutes per weekly set, mostly checking, 2.5 hours in total, roughly 1.8 staff-minutes per student. Each podcast reached more than 70 unique listeners (about 82% of the cohort). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
  - Limitations the authors state: one course with one pre-period cohort, so parallel trends cannot be tested; possible effort substitution between halves; no individual usage logs; verification is bundled with new material, so its separate effect is not identified. The data cannot be shared under the ethics approval. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07097)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: n = 85 per cell, so tail estimates rest on a handful of students. The "judgement burden" mechanism is plausible but untested: there was no unverified arm, which the authors themselves propose as the next experiment. The interview evidence is field notes, not transcripts. The authors warn that a label can keep signalling after the checking behind it gets cursory, and propose a verification log as the fix.
- Mia hook: "What's the equivalent of the named TA for an unattended pipeline like this podcast? Is a primary/secondary tag per claim a verification label, and does it make the listener check more or less?"

## Deep dive: Your eval has a shelf life: rater effects, drift and how often to re-evaluate a deployed agent
- Why this topic, why now: Lucas wants agents that run on their own with his role kept small, and that only works if the evidence that an agent works stays current. A new econ.GN working paper (Zhang & Esposito, arXiv 2610.07003) studies exactly this on a real production agent with human raters, a changelog and support tickets. It uses tools economists know (fixed effects with connected sets, a local-level state-space model, control-chart economics) and turns them into rules any agent builder can apply. It builds on, but differs from, the 6 October deep dive on LLM-judge scale validation: the question here is whether scores stay comparable over time, and how long an evaluation stays valid.
- Outline:
  1. **The setting and the problem.** Zara is a voice-and-video agent that runs live job interviews for micro1, at roughly 32,000–91,000 interviews a week. Trainers scored 2,611 production recordings in 14 roughly fortnightly batches (26 March–24 August 2026) against a nine-family rubric. The agent changes, the users change and the raters change, so a raw score trend mixes all three. The paper models reliability as a latent state θ: each score = state + rater severity (γ_r) + noise. The state moves through deliberate repairs (deploys) plus unlogged noise η with variance q per week.
  2. **Mechanism 1: a roster change can hide a real improvement.** Reviewer B joined on 21 May and scored the same batches 0.79 SD lower than reviewer A on the stability index. Batch fixed effects explain 1.4% of score variance; adding reviewer fixed effects raises that to 16.2%. In other words, who scored a recording predicts its score better than when the interview happened. The raw trend was +0.002 SD/month (p = 0.93). The reviewer-adjusted trend was +0.102 SD/month (p < 0.001), and +0.124 using reviewer A alone. From 9 May to 10 August the improvement and the composition shift were about the same size (adjusted +0.48, composition −0.34), so they cancelled in the raw series. Identification is the AKM/judge-fixed-effects argument: raters must overlap within batches, so the rater-by-batch graph is connected. Severity is a vector, not a scalar: −1.40 SD on latency but −0.13 on conversation structure. Raters disagree on judgements of degree ("how long a pause is too long") and agree on events ("did the video freeze"). Even B's strictness flips on the critical-failure flag (2.0% vs A's 5.0%). The authors say the same logic applies to LLM judges: "a judge version is a rater".
  3. **Mechanism 2: an index hides opposite movements.** Conversation-structure deductions fell from 63% to 23% of interviews (−9.3 pp/month, the only family that survives Holm correction). Four families did not move, and most families sit at ceiling more than 90% of the time. The 12 April deploy raised the index by 0.20 SD and structure by 0.37 SD, but critical failures went up 4.7 pp. The smoothed critical-failure rate went from 2.8% to a 6.3% peak (around 9 May) and back to 2.5%. A vendor model launch on 23 April serves as a placebo date (index +0.02), because production pinned fixed version names. Rule: report families, not just the index. A separate staging-channel episode adds a twist: the holistic 1–10 impression fell from 8.5 to 3.9 while the checklist barely moved. The regression lay outside the rubric, which argues for keeping one holistic item.
  4. **Mechanism 3: drift, and how long evidence stays current.** A two-stage estimator: (1) batch effects net of reviewer fixed effects; (2) a local-level model θ̂_t = μ_t + v_t, μ_{t+1} = μ_t + η_t, with the innovation variance q fitted by profile maximum likelihood. The weekly innovation SD is 0.075 for all transitions and 0.086 for movement not explained by deploys. Freeing the deploy transitions barely changes it, so most movement happened between batches with no deploy. Freeing a late-May step of 0.34 SD that has no commit behind it brings the remaining drift to about 0. The index moved in a few jumps, not continuously. Candidate sources are configuration outside the repo, silent vendor updates behind a fixed model name, or a population shift (software roles rose from 17% to 27% of interviews). Forecast variance grows as P_T + q·h. The forecast SD reaches the largest deploy contrast (0.20 SD) after about 5 weeks, with a 95% range of 1–21 weeks: after that, without a new evaluation, you can no longer tell whether a deploy-sized change happened. For critical failures, a quarter without evaluation leaves you unsure whether you are at the top or bottom of the observed 4-point range.
  5. **Mechanism 4: link the evaluation to an outcome you didn't design, and price it.** Interview-related tickets, set against technical-issue tickets on the same dashboard as a control, fell about 10% per month (t = −2.69). Per interview the fall was 7% per month, and 18% per month for June–August. This shows direction only: complaints are a selected signal. The paper is explicit that evaluation did not cause the improvement; there is no unevaluated arm. Evaluation only has value if repair can respond (Proposition 1). Here findings arrived every fortnight but production changed three times in five months, which suggests repair capacity, not information, was the bottleneck. Proposition 2 gives the optimal evaluation interval as h* ≈ √(2c/(κq)), the logic of the economic design of control charts (Duncan 1956), so "evaluate continuously" is only optimal as drift → ∞ or cost → 0. Pricing: rater pay was $7,806/month against about 428 fewer escalations per month. At $100 per escalation, the program pays for itself if it deserves about 18% of the credit.
  6. **What carries over to small, single-builder setups.** Treat every judge (a human, an LLM judge, or the same LLM judge after a model update) as a rater with its own severity per criterion. Keep an overlapping anchor set so that judge changes are identified within batch. Log everything that can move the state, including prompts, model aliases and config, so that unexplained drift really is unexplained. Pin dated model snapshots. Estimate your own q rather than picking a cadence by feel.
- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Authors Liu Zhang and Mark Esposito; working paper, October 2026, "preliminary; comments welcome"; JEL C38, D83, L86, M15, O33. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - 2,611 production recordings, 14 batches of 68–335 recordings, the last one partial (35); nine rubric families; reviewer A scored all batches, reviewer B from 21 May (about half of each batch), reviewer C one batch of 47. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Reviewer B vs A: −0.792 SD (SE 0.045) on the index. Per-batch gap ranges from −0.90 to −0.53; pooled −0.77; heterogeneity Q = 19.0 (p = 0.015), so additivity is an approximation. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Latent index rose from 0.05 SD (29 March) to 0.58 SD (10 August), +0.53 (SE 0.07). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Deploy contrasts (two batches after minus two before): 12 April +0.20 SD index, +4.70 pp critical failure; 16 July −0.00; 29 July +0.12 (structure +0.32); vendor launch placebo +0.02. The authors read these as magnitudes, not tests, because each rests on four batches. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Drift (weekly innovation SD): index 0.075 [0.041, 0.142] for all transitions; 0.086 [0.041, 0.180] with deploys freed; 0.000 (p = 0.50) with the late-May step also freed. Critical failures: 0.93, 0.76, 0.67 pp. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Forecast SD of the index is 0.18 after 4 weeks, 0.25 after 8 and 0.30 after 12. It reaches 0.20 after about 5 weeks (6 with the pooled rate), with a 1–21 week range. At the fortnightly cadence the forecast SD is about half a large deploy contrast. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - A recording defect (a mid-session reconnect started a second recording that overwrote the first) may have inflated audio-visual sync failures, which make up 73% of critical failures, for much of the window. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Commits did not cluster after evaluation sign-off dates (63% within 7 days vs a 67% permutation null, p = 0.73), and no commit cites an evaluation finding. To identify the causal effect you would need a contemporaneous record of which finding motivated each commit, plus designed variation in evaluation intensity. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - Program cost $7,806/month in rater pay, excluding engineering time. Break-even credit share: 73% at $25 per escalation, 36% at $50, 18% at $100, 7% at $250. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - The authors call the local-level model "a few lines of code" that gives each firm its own number. An agent with fully version-controlled config and dated vendor snapshots should show less unexplained movement; one with a floating model alias or a changing user population, more. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
  - The authors do not say whether code or data will be released. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07003)
- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week: Pick one running pipeline, for example Morning Signal's research step or a RAG app.
  - (a) Freeze an anchor set of 30–50 items and score it with every judge you use: yourself, the current LLM judge, and any new judge model or prompt version. Estimate a per-criterion judge offset before comparing scores across weeks.
  - (b) Start a changelog that captures prompt edits, model IDs (pin dated snapshots where possible) and config, not just code commits.
  - (c) Score a small weekly batch. Fit a local-level model (for example statsmodels `UnobservedComponents(level='local level')`) on the judge-adjusted batch means to get q. Then compute the shelf life s = (D² − P₀)/q, where D is the smallest change you would act on, and set your evaluation cadence from that.
  - (d) Choose one outcome the judge never sees as corroboration: his own correction rate, the share of items he skips, or downstream errors found.
- Mia hooks:
  - "I switched my LLM judge from one model version to the next and my RAG faithfulness score dropped six points overnight. Nothing in the pipeline changed. Is that the 'reviewer B joins' story, and how many overlap items do I need to separate the two?"
  - "The jump with no commit behind it is the scary part: config outside the repo, or a vendor quietly updating a model behind the same alias. Do you pin dated snapshots, and do you log your prompts like code?"
  - "Five weeks sounds precise, but the interval runs from 1 to 21 weeks on 14 batches. Is a shelf-life estimate that wide still useful for deciding cadence, or should you just evaluate after every change plus a fixed heartbeat?"

- Summary: Anthropic's Haiku 5.5 cuts prices tenfold with a tokenizer and 100k-token catch, OpenAI dumps 722 machine-made math manuscripts, Anthropic ties cyber permissions to verified tiers, and a King's College study finds verified AI study podcasts help weaker economics students most.
