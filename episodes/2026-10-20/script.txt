GEORGE: Which brings us to memory, the useful kind. IBM Research published ALTK-Evolve, and the idea is almost embarrassingly simple. A model, GPT-4.1 or the base model itself, reads agent trajectories on training tasks and distils the successes and failures into short behavioural rules. Those rules get appended to the system prompt, after the domain policy. No weight updates, no human annotation, no vector store.
MIA: So it's a lessons-learned list, written by the model.
GEORGE: Precisely. The clever bit is a "support" count: the number of distinct training tasks a guideline was extracted from. They run two settings. "Sup three" keeps guidelines with support of three or more. "Lossless" keeps everything with support of at least one. In retail that's thirty-one guidelines versus two hundred and ninety-two. In airline, fourteen versus a hundred and one.
MIA: And the results?
GEORGE: On tau-squared bench retail, GPT-4.1, forty held-out tasks. Mean accuracy goes from seventy-eight point one to eighty-two point five. On the full leaderboard split, sixty-seven and a half ranks number one, ahead of RAFT-30B-A3B at sixty-one point four, which was fine-tuned on synthetic retail data for this very benchmark.
MIA: A prompt beating a fine-tune. I like that. Airline?
GEORGE: With GPT-5.5 as the agent, pass-hat-four goes from fifty-five to seventy percent, and the full-split score of eighty-two percent ranks first, above frontier entries at sixty-six to seventy-two. MIA: So the right dose depends on the domain. Another knob to tune. What does it cost?

MIA: TRACE. Amirhossein Zohrehvand, who goes by Miros, at LIACS in Leiden, and Aldona Kapačinskaitė at INSEAD. Posted on arXiv on the sixteenth of October, in general economics. And it goes straight at your question, Lucas: how do you let agents do substantive research work while your own role stays small but decisive?
GEORGE: Their framing of the problem is what grabbed me. The issue isn't that AI produces too little, it's that it produces too much. They cite Gartenberg and colleagues, who studied six thousand nine hundred and fifty-seven initial submissions and ten thousand three hundred and eighty-nine reviews at Organization Science. After ChatGPT, submission volume rose forty-two percent while measured writing quality fell, and AI-associated writing accounted for nearly all of both trends.
MIA: More papers, worse papers. An economist would call that a congestion externality, with reviewers paying for it.
GEORGE: And their key observation: research is coauthored. One person's AI output becomes another person's input. So it's not a personal productivity problem, it's a collective governance problem. Who chose what, what was checked against what, and who accepted it?
MIA: Before the principles, I want to talk about how they derived them, because the method is itself an AI research pipeline worth stealing.
GEORGE: Go on, you'll explain it better than me, you build these things.
MIA: In the spirit of Ostrom's design principles, they searched fourteen top software-engineering and security outlets plus Scopus-wide reviews. Ten thousand three hundred and twenty-two records. LLMs did the screening: Claude Opus 5 first, Haiku 5.5 as a second screener, and Sonnet 5.5 resolving nine hundred and fifty-two conflicts. That left one thousand five hundred and sixty-five papers.
GEORGE: Then extraction.
MIA: Two models separately extracted actions, each with a supporting quote. Then a plain script dropped every action whose quote didn't appear in the paper. A hundred and sixty-two went out that way, leaving sixteen thousand two hundred and seventy-two actions.
GEORGE: That's the bit I love. String-matching the quote against the source is about the cheapest hallucination guard there is. No model involved at all.
MIA: Then a triple filter. An action stayed only if both models said yes to "applies to research", "applies with AI", and "one party's work becomes another's input". Down to two thousand two hundred and sixty-five. Those were restated without jargon, embedded with the BGE base English model, clustered with k-means into fifty-six activities, consensus-sorted into eight families, and those became five principles covering ninety-six percent of the actions.
GEORGE: They also checked the jargon-free rewrites across models: cosine similarity of zero point eight four between the two models' versions, against zero point five five for unrelated pairs. So two things to copy: dual-model agreement as a filter, and verbatim quote matching.
MIA: Here's my issue though. I do the quote match in my RAG extraction too, and it misses paraphrased hallucinations. The quote is real, but the claim drawn from it isn't what the quote says. What's the next cheapest check?
GEORGE: An entailment check against the matched quote, done by a different model than the extractor. You're no longer asking "is this true?", which is open-ended, but "does this specific quote support this specific claim?", which is narrow. Narrow yes-or-no questions are where small, cheap models are actually reliable.
MIA: And they ran an alternative derivation as a robustness check.
GEORGE: Yes. Claude Opus 5 agents grouped practices into four hundred and fifty categories. MIA: And a full rerun costs about a hundred dollars at Anthropic list prices, half with batch. They saved the model answers too, so readers can recompute the figures without rerunning anything.
GEORGE: Which is a nice replication-package idea in its own right. Right, the five principles. I'll try to describe them as mechanisms rather than slogans.
MIA: Please.
GEORGE: One: track records. Keep the instruction in the researcher's own words, the inputs, the agent's consequential choices with reasons, and the version that ran. Their example: an agent sets the top income bracket at a hundred and fifty thousand. Months later, only the record says why.
MIA: That one hits home. Every coding choice in a data pipeline is quietly a research choice.
GEORGE: Two: review against sources. Check an output against a named criterion, the regression table, the cited paper, the analysis plan, not against the AI's own account of its work. And naming the criterion tells others what a pass covers. "Numbers match the data" is a very different pass from "the script runs".
MIA: Which is the NBER finding in one sentence.
GEORGE: Three: accept independently. The producer never accepts its own work. And the subtle part: a second model's critique counts as advice, not acceptance. A researcher can delegate acceptance to an agent or a process "within stated limits", but stays accountable.
MIA: Their example is painfully familiar. An agent revises a theory section, silently drops a citation and reorders the hypotheses, and acceptance is where those unrequested choices surface. I had exactly that: an agent revised a section after coauthor comments, and I noticed weeks later it had dropped a reference. But honestly, would an acceptance queue have caught it, or would I just click accept like I do with cookie banners?
GEORGE: That's the real risk. An acceptance step that shows you a diff of a whole section is a cookie banner. One that says "unrequested changes: removed one citation, swapped hypotheses two and three" is a decision. So the design burden is making the agent list its own unrequested choices, and then checking that list mechanically, say by diffing the citation keys before and after.
MIA: So the agent declares, and a script checks the declaration.
GEORGE: Exactly. Four: control transitions. Keep exploratory work separate from work others rely on, and move material only at defined crossings: outside data coming in, a draft going into the manuscript, an instruction becoming a standing rule. Then when a problem shows up, you can trace it to the crossing where it entered.
MIA: Like customs. Nothing gets into the country except at a border post.
GEORGE: Nice. And five: evaluate corrections. An error stays open until a targeted recheck shows the fix worked. And if the error came from a standing instruction, you fix the instruction too, and recheck the later work produced under it. Their example: a wrong denominator in one appendix table probably affects every table built from the same code.
MIA: For anyone running agents, that's the big one. If a bug came from my CLAUDE dot md file, every output since that line went in is suspect.
GEORGE: For weight: track records covers thirty-five percent of the actions, review against sources twenty-two point seven, accept independently seventeen point two, control transitions twelve point three, and evaluate corrections eight point seven.
MIA: Now TRACE-OS, the implementation. This is where it gets practical.
GEORGE: And refreshingly boring, in a good way. Plain files, Git, and Python 3.11 standard-library scripts. No server, no database, no model calls in the record tools. The folders draw the boundaries. Sources holds durable evidence and judgments, plus the intake and transition registries. Workspace slash sandbox is where the agent writes by default. Paper is the only folder synced to Overleaf. And the AI folder holds trace records, acceptances, the defect ledger and agentic reviews.
MIA: So the control-transitions principle is literally a folder boundary. The agent plays in the sandbox, and moving something into paper is a crossing.
GEORGE: Right. Every commit is authored by the researcher and carries a Record line and an Execution-agent line naming the model. And acceptance is "declared, never inferred".
MIA: Meaning a merge isn't acceptance, and silence certainly isn't.
GEORGE: Exactly. Work sits in one of seven states: not reviewed, reviewed by a model, accepted by a person, accepted by a delegated actor, returned for correction, refused, and reviewed by an undeclared actor. Only two of those count as acceptance.
MIA: I like that "reviewed by a model" is explicitly not acceptance. That's the trap most of us fall into: the second agent said it's fine, so it's fine.
GEORGE: The defect ledger has the same discipline. A fix moves an entry to "fixed". Only a recheck that names its criterion, method, outcome, checker and limits moves it to "verified". And the agentic reviewer's quotes are checked against the reviewed file, the same trick as in the derivation.
MIA: And the skills?
GEORGE: Ten. One per principle, a trace-check across all five, plus claim-evidence-audit, literature-processing, meeting-and-idea-intake, and a Zotero knowledge base skill. MIA: And installing it?
GEORGE: One instruction to the agent: read the SETUP-AGENT file. The agent shows a plan of every file before writing, leaves conflicting files untouched, pushes nothing and creates no accounts.
MIA: Which is a nice example of a well-behaved agent installer in itself.

MIA: And their line, "pace AI-assisted work to human attention rather than to the speed at which AI produces content". That's the economist's point. My attention is the binding constraint, so the system should be designed around it, not around the agent's throughput.
GEORGE: In practice that means bounded units of work, an acceptance queue that shows the agent's choices, and delegated acceptance for low-stakes classes of work. MIA: So the hard question. Delegated acceptance "within stated limits". What limits would you actually write down for an agent that accepts its own data-cleaning work overnight?
