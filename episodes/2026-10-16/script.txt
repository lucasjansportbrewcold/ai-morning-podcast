GEORGE: So first, why bits matter. To generate one token, the model has to read all its active weights from memory. So decode speed is roughly memory bandwidth divided by the bytes of active weights. Halve the bytes, roughly double the speed. Capacity is a separate constraint: memory has to hold all the weights, active or not, plus the KV cache.
MIA: So with mixture of experts, total parameters decide whether it fits, and active parameters decide how fast it goes.
GEORGE: Exactly. Muse is about one and a half terabytes at sixteen-bit, and still three hundred and forty-four gigabytes at three to four bits. MAI Code, with a hundred and thirty-seven billion total, lands at about fifty-three gigabytes. Bolt, seventy-four billion total and nine billion active, targets twenty-four.
MIA: And then the KV cache, which everyone forgets.
GEORGE: Muse needs about two hundred and fifty kilobytes of cache per token at sixteen-bit. Times a million tokens, the context alone is about two hundred and fifty gigabytes. That's why the serving recipe caps context at a hundred and thirty-one thousand tokens on eight RTX Pro 6000 cards, and you only get the full million on eight H200s.
MIA: So "one million context" on the spec sheet and on your hardware are different things. And prefill is the other half. Muse prefills at two hundred and twenty-five tokens a second on that Mac. GEORGE: Unless the runtime caches the prefix, so only the new tokens get processed each step. Prefill is limited by compute rather than memory, which is why it's so much faster than generation. Microsoft quotes nine hundred and twenty-three and a half tokens a second for MAI Code on a Surface Laptop Ultra at sixty-four thousand tokens of context, and seven hundred and sixty-nine point eight at a hundred and twenty-eight thousand. Decode is the bandwidth-bound part, and that's where fewer bits buy you speed.
MIA: Okay. So what does "four-bit" actually mean, mechanically?
GEORGE: Take NVIDIA's NVFP4 format. Each value gets four bits: one sign bit, two exponent bits, one mantissa bit. That's a tiny set of values, roughly minus six to plus six.
MIA: Which is nothing.
GEORGE: On its own, nothing. The trick is scaling. Every sixteen values share an eight-bit floating-point scale, and there's a thirty-two-bit scale for the whole tensor on top. Think of photographing a scene with very bright and very dark areas. One exposure for the whole picture loses detail. Metering each small patch separately keeps most of it.
MIA: And the competing format?
GEORGE: MXFP4 uses blocks of thirty-two, and scales that can only be powers of two. Bigger patches, coarser exposure, more error. NVFP4 costs about four and a half bits per value once you count the scales: three and a half times smaller than sixteen-bit, one point eight times smaller than eight-bit.
MIA: And Bolt's two bits?
GEORGE: Its experts use a GGUF format called IQ2 XXS, at two point oh six two five bits per element. But nobody quantizes uniformly any more. Bolt keeps experts at two bits, its Mamba layers at NVFP4, and embeddings and output at eight bits. NVIDIA's QAD runs keep attention layers and their neighbours at sixteen-bit.
MIA: Makes sense. The experts are most of the weights, so that's where the savings are, and attention is small but everything flows through it.
GEORGE: Right. Now the three ways to get there. First, post-training quantization, PTQ. No training at all: you push a small calibration set through the model to set the scales, and you're done. Essentially free. And for very large models it works remarkably well. DeepSeek R1, six hundred and seventy-one billion parameters, from its official eight-bit to NVFP4: GPQA Diamond goes from sixty-nine point seven to sixty-nine point two, and AIME twenty-four stays at eighty. NVIDIA puts the loss at one percent or less across seven benchmarks.
MIA: But.
GEORGE: But smaller post-trained reasoning models lose much more. Llama Nemotron Super V1 drops from forty-six to thirty-two point three on AIME twenty-five. Nearly a third of its maths, gone. And NVIDIA notes the classic tricks for outlier weights, rotations and smoothing, stop helping at sixteen-value blocks. The blocks are already small enough that there's little left for those tricks to fix.
MIA: So big models have the redundancy to absorb the noise, small ones don't. Then the obvious fix is to train the model to cope.
GEORGE: That's quantization-aware training, QAT. You fine-tune with quantized weights and activations in the forward pass, so the model learns to live with the rounding, using the usual next-token cross-entropy loss. And here's the problem. A modern model is supervised fine-tuning, then reinforcement learning, then merges. You can't replay that pipeline. So people fall back on supervised data, which pulls the model back toward its pre-RL behaviour.
MIA: And on RL-heavy models it was worse than doing nothing, if I read the paper right.
GEORGE: You did. AceReason Nemotron 7B on AIME twenty-five: original sixty-three point five, PTQ fifty-eight point seven, QAT forty-six point one. Seventeen points below the original.
MIA: The fix did more damage than the problem.
GEORGE: And the diagnosis is lovely. QAT matched the original's cross-entropy almost exactly, zero point four oh eight. But its KL divergence from the original model was zero point three one one. Same loss, different model. In effect it was an extra post-training stage that quietly undid some of what RL had taught.
MIA: That's a lesson well beyond quantization. Matching the loss doesn't mean matching the behaviour.
GEORGE: Which brings us to quantization-aware distillation, QAD. Instead of training the quantized model on labels, you train it to match the full-precision original's output distribution. Teacher and student are the same model, one at sixteen-bit, one at four. The loss is KL divergence at temperature one.
MIA: You distil the model into its own quantized self.
GEORGE: Exactly. Because the student copies the teacher's full distribution token by token, it inherits what RL taught without replaying RL. KL to the original drops to zero point oh oh four, against QAT's zero point three one one. AceReason gets sixty-two against the original's sixty-three point five. Llama Nemotron Super, forty-five point six against forty-six, where PTQ gave thirty-two point three. And on long-context reasoning, Nemotron 3 Nano on the AA LCR benchmark: original thirty-five point nine, PTQ thirty-one point three, QAT twenty-four point eight, QAD thirty-four point three.
MIA: QAT losing eleven points on long-context reasoning is the scary number for anyone doing RAG.
GEORGE: Agreed. And QAD is cheap by training standards, about zero point three to six billion tokens depending on the model.
MIA: Now the part that confused me. Training on random tokens didn't break the model, and keeping wrong answers in the data mostly helped. That feels backwards.
GEORGE: Until you ask what's being transferred. In ordinary fine-tuning, the data is the signal: you're teaching answers. In distillation, the teacher is the signal, and the input is just a probe. Whatever text you feed in, even random tokens, the teacher produces a full probability distribution over the next token, and the student learns to match it. A wrong answer is still a faithful record of the teacher's behaviour.
MIA: So the data only has to visit the right regions of behaviour. It doesn't have to be correct.
GEORGE: Right. Training only on code recovered maths just as well: AIME twenty-five at sixty-two, the same as with the full data. Model-generated data works.
