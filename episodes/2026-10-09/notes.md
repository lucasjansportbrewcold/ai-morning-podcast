## News
### 1. AgentCorruption: one chat message to a public Bedrock agent opened up every agent in the AWS account
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is what goes wrong when a cloud agent has an HTTP or shell tool and a broad default role. Prompt injection is only the way in. The real damage comes from the agent's credentials. It is a concrete checklist item for anyone hosting agents: block the metadata endpoint, scope the execution role, treat memory as an attack surface.
- Facts:
  - Zenity Labs told the agent, through its `http_request` tool, to fetch `http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/`, the Instance Metadata Service (IMDS). It reproduced the same access through the `shell` tool with curl, which shows the problem sat in the platform, not in one tool. [primary] (https://labs.zenity.io/post/agentcorruption-initial-imds-access)
  - Through IMDS they got full temporary AWS STS credentials (access key ID, secret key, session token), the role name `execution_role`, the agent's container image URI, mTLS certificate material and a presigned S3 URL to internal AWS infrastructure. [primary] (https://labs.zenity.io/post/agentcorruption-initial-imds-access)
  - The default execution role was broad: ECR image pulls on `repository/*`, `logs:DescribeLogGroups` (which reveals every agent in the region), and read/write/delete across several AgentCore services. [primary] (https://labs.zenity.io/post/agentcorruption-initial-imds-access)
  - Disclosure timeline: reported to AWS on 17 December 2025. AWS made IMDSv2-only the enforced default for newly deployed agents from 14 February 2026, and closed the report as "informative" on 12 April 2026. [primary] (https://labs.zenity.io/post/agentcorruption-initial-imds-access)
  - With the stolen credentials the researchers could reach every AgentCore agent in the same account and region. They could download agent source code, read private conversations and access stored passwords and API keys. For agents with long-term memory, they could plant instructions to forward future conversations to the attacker. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/a-single-prompt-was-enough-to-hijack-every-ai-agent-in-an-aws-account-zenity-researchers-found/)
  - AWS later changed the default execution role so agents can no longer invoke other agents, read conversations or pull Secrets Manager credentials. Zenity still recommends custom, narrower roles. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/a-single-prompt-was-enough-to-hijack-every-ai-agent-in-an-aws-account-zenity-researchers-found/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The fixes apply to newly deployed agents. Agents deployed earlier with the old defaults may still have the broad role unless someone redeploys them or tightens the role by hand. The memory-poisoning step is the scary part: the compromise survives after the credentials expire. IMDS credential theft is an old SSRF pattern from the cloud world. The new part is that an LLM with an HTTP tool is an SSRF engine you can steer in plain language.
- Mia hook: "I checked my own agent container: one curl to 169.254.169.254 from the code tool and it answered. What's the minimum: an egress deny rule for link-local addresses, or a separate role per agent?"

### 2. Silicon samples, tested like a stated-preference survey: coherent is not the same as correct
- Why it matters for Lucas: If you use LLMs as simulated respondents or as stand-ins for valuations, this paper gives an economist's toolkit for validating output when there is no ground truth. It shows newer models can pass every theory-based test and still be off by a factor of 4 to 6 against humans.
- Facts:
  - Daniel Robert Kling Alexander (University of Michigan School of Information) and Catherine Louise Kling (Cornell) carry stated-preference validity concepts (content, construct and criterion validity, reliability, incentive compatibility, consequentiality) over to LLM evaluation. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - They gave the water-quality referendum survey of Vossler et al. (2023) to six models: Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4o and GPT-5.6 Terra. Design: nine scenarios, ten tax levels from $20 to $3,000 a year, household incomes of $35,000, $75,000 and $200,000, ten draws each, 16,200 votes in total. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - Demand slope: Claude Haiku 4.5 voted yes in all 600 first-round draws, even at $3,000 a year. GPT-4o mini voted yes 87% of the time at $3,000. Sonnet 5 and GPT-5.6 Terra had clean downward slopes, reaching 0.00 and 0.08 yes votes at $3,000. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - Spatial-extent test: Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-4o priced the full region below the local watershed in 5 of 8 comparisons. Income effects never ran the wrong way in 69 scored comparisons, but many $200,000 values are censored above $3,000. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - Format dependence: Haiku 4.5's open-ended median WTP was $575 to $1,650 a year, yet it voted yes at $3,000 every time. Letting models reason before answering multiplied Haiku's answers by 2.8x and GPT-4o's by 2.3x. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - At $75,000 income, Sonnet 5's WTP was about 4x the human means and GPT-5.6 Terra's about 6x. Their income elasticities of 1.0 to 2.2 are far above what stated-preference studies usually find (well below 1.0). [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10506v1)
  - The authors' own conclusion: "Passing validity tests shows that a model's answers are coherent, not that they are correct." [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10506)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: One survey and one good. Ten draws per cell give standard errors of about 0.15 near a 50% yes share. The newest models were run under undated provider-default names, so they may have changed between runs. Option order was not swapped. Models may have learned what textbook survey answers look like from training data. Incentive compatibility and consequentiality were not tested, and they are arguably the core of stated-preference validity. Fits with Ingar Haaland's prediction (via Marginal Revolution) that work which can be fully outsourced to AI, such as simple survey experiments, is "in for a big shock" (https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/10/predictions-for-economics-given-ai.html) [primary].
- Mia hook: "So a scope test is basically a unit test for a silicon sample. What would your checklist be before you trust any LLM-simulated WTP number in a policy note?"

### 3. Epoch: the price of a given level of AI performance falls about 13x a year
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is economics-of-AI evidence with a clear method, and it matters for build decisions. If the same capability costs a tenth as much a year from now, an automation idea that is marginal at today's prices can be worth designing now.
- Facts:
  - David Roodman and Luke Emberson estimate that the cost of reaching a fixed level of performance has fallen about 47% per quarter (about 13x per year) since 2023. [primary] (https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought)
  - Method: five benchmarks (AIME/OTIS Mock, Chess Puzzles, FrontierMath tiers 1-3, GPQA Diamond, Mystery Game Puzzles). They trace each model's cost-performance Pareto frontier by simulating lower token budgets from transcripts of high-budget runs (CAISI methodology). [primary] (https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought)
  - Declines are fastest at the state of the art: 66% per quarter at first, slowing to 32% per quarter two years later. [primary] (https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought)
  - Compared with other technologies, the decline is 4x faster than DNA sequencing (1.84x a year), 6x faster than compute (1.51x), 18x faster than lithium batteries (1.16x) and 54x faster than electricity (1.05x, 1892-1973). [primary] (https://epoch.ai/publications/the-plunging-price-of-thought)
  - Same Epoch brief: InnovationEval, with a 3,000 GPU-hour budget, found no model making meaningful post-training breakthroughs, so AI R&D is "not yet" automated. [secondary] (https://epochai.substack.com/p/the-epoch-brief-october-8-2026)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The authors flag the caveats themselves. Benchmarks may be trained toward ("benchmaxxing"). Real users don't switch to the cheapest model on the frontier. Three years is a short window. Different averaging methods give 42.9% to 58% per quarter. The benchmarks are math, science and games, not agentic work with long contexts. Yesterday's Haiku 5.5 story, with its hungrier tokenizer and a price cliff at 100k tokens, shows that list price per token and cost per task can move apart.
- Mia hook: "Price per benchmark point drops 13x a year, yet my monthly agent bill went up. Is that the Jevons effect or just longer contexts?"

### 4. CrowdStrike: a likely single attacker used an open-source agentic pentest stack against Korean banks
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is the other side of autonomous agents. A single operator with an off-the-shelf agentic toolchain is now a realistic threat for mid-size institutions, including Dutch SMEs and public bodies.
- Facts:
  - CrowdStrike describes a campaign from late September to early October 2026 against several South Korean financial organisations, including at least two banks whose loan-inquiry and employee mobile work-support systems were breached. [primary] (https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/unknown-threat-actor-uses-artex-to-target-south-korean-finance/)
  - The attacker used ARTEX, an open-source agentic penetration-testing tool developed in China, with DeepSeek v4.1-flash as the main backend, plus GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6. Claude Code sessions directed reconnaissance and vulnerability research. [primary] (https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/unknown-threat-actor-uses-artex-to-target-south-korean-finance/)
  - Infrastructure: a Hong Kong server controlled an ARTEX instance in the US. CrowdStrike assesses with moderate confidence that the actor is a financially motivated Chinese speaker, partly based on Chinese-language prompts. [primary] (https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/unknown-threat-actor-uses-artex-to-target-south-korean-finance/)
  - The Decoder reports that more than 25,000 customer records were stolen at Shinhan Bank alone. [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/ai-powered-hacking-tools-enabled-a-likely-single-attacker-to-breach-multiple-south-korean-banks/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: CrowdStrike does not disclose the initial access vector, so we don't know whether the AI found new holes or mainly sped up known ones. "Single person" is an inference. Vendor threat reports also have a commercial interest in dramatic framing.

## Deep dive: Verify, don't opine. When should a manager agent send work back?
- Why this topic, why now: A Leiden University (LIACS) paper, revised on 7 October, runs a clean paired experiment on the default multi-agent pattern where a manager can reject a worker's output and order a revision. Revision made reports worse, cost 51.5% more tokens, and the damage traced entirely to the revision step. Lucas builds pipelines with review loops (including this podcast's own research and script stages), so the question "when does a loop-back pay for itself?" applies to him directly. Not covered before: earlier deep dives were about LLM judges and eval shelf life, not about coordination structure.
- Outline:
  1. **The design question.** Frameworks give a Manager agent loop-back authority by default: it reviews the state and can issue binding "fix this" directives. Organisation theory predicts that authority forces convergence and polish. The LLM literature predicts the opposite: RLHF-trained workers respond to authority with compliance, not belief revision. The paper's framing is that an authority link is also a conditioning signal on the worker's next generation, not just a control channel.
  2. **The experiment.** Five roles (Researcher with ChromaDB retrieval, Analyst, Writer, Critic with tool-grounded checks, Manager) write business-intelligence reports on 43 gaming laptops from specs and reviews. Each product is run once flat (Manager as non-binding peer router) and once hierarchical (binding directives, at most 2 revision loops). That gives 86 runs, with models randomly assigned to roles from GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Qwen-3.5-122B, GLM-5 and Mistral-Large-3. The task is partly verifiable: specs can be checked against listings, analysis cannot.
  3. **The result.** Flat beats hierarchical on Utility (4.715 vs 4.621, d=0.42, p=0.009) and Writing Clarity (4.454 vs 4.360, d=0.34, p=0.030). Spec accuracy is identical and at ceiling. The hierarchical version uses 53% more hedging words per 1,000 words (5.03 vs 3.30) at the same length, 51.5% more tokens, 40.5% higher cost and 34.3% more wall time (282 vs 210 s).
  4. **The mechanism: revision, not hierarchy.** Hierarchical first drafts cannot be told apart from flat reports. The gap opens after the first revision: the second draft adds 1.95 hedges per 1,000 words while keeping 88% of the common subsequence. The Writer complies by hedging and padding, not by rethinking. Clarity falls with each loop (OLS β=-0.142 per loop). Runs where the Manager never used its authority showed no harm. This matches older findings: intrinsic self-correction without external feedback often fails or makes things worse (Huang et al.), and preference-optimised models drift toward telling the evaluator what it wants to hear (Sharma et al.).
  5. **When review loops do work.** The Kamoi et al. TACL survey finds that self-correction works when reliable external feedback exists. CRITIC shows gains when the critique comes from tools: search for fact checks, a code interpreter for bugs. The paper's design rule: "A Manager with loop-back authority earns its cost when it can verify something and becomes a liability when it can only opine." Trigger revisions on verifier failures (a failed claim check, a missing section, a failing test), not on a manager's taste.
  6. **How strong is the evidence?** One short synthesis task, one model generation, one Manager prompt, 43 pairs. Judge agreement is low (Krippendorff's alpha 0.08-0.17 for clarity, 0.22-0.30 for utility). The blind human check (20 pairs, 2 raters) points the same way but is not significant (flat wins 54%, p=0.75). The number of loops is self-selected by the Manager, so the dose-response is correlational. The hedge lexicon cannot separate calibrated uncertainty from evasion. Utility is the robust result: all 5 judges favour flat, and it survives leaving any judge out. Clarity is fragile.
- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Authors Burak Agachan, Max van Duijn and Amirhossein Zohrehvand, all at LIACS, Leiden University. Submitted 13 September 2026, revised 7 October 2026. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14767)
  - Design: 43 products with at least 5 reviews, 86 runs, hierarchical arm capped at 2 revision loops. Five-model LLM judge panel scoring analysis first, then 1-5. Deterministic spec checking via regex plus vector-store verification. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Utility 4.715 vs 4.621 (d=0.42, p=0.009). Writing Clarity 4.454 vs 4.360 (d=0.34, p=0.030). Spec accuracy d=-0.046, p=0.764. Flat wins 28 of 43 products, hierarchical 14, 1 tie. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - The clarity gap is driven by Conciseness (4.18 vs 3.98, d=0.44); the utility gap by Strategic Depth (4.70 vs 4.56, d=0.48). Structure and Coherence show no difference. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Hedging: 5.03 vs 3.30 per 1,000 words (dz=0.61, p<0.001). Length 1,036 vs 1,046 words (p=0.76). Lexical diversity equal. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Cost: 74,781 vs 49,370 tokens (+51.5%), +40.5% generation cost, 282 vs 210 seconds. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Dose-response: clarity is about 4.48 at zero loops and 3.79 for cap-blocked three-loop runs. Spearman ρ=-0.40 (p=0.008). Per loop β=-0.142 (p<0.001); β=-0.074 (p=0.038) after dropping cap-blocked runs. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - First hierarchical drafts match flat reports on hedging (dz=0.11, p=0.46). Revision keeps 88% of the common subsequence and 85% of the vocabulary. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Authors' recommendation: default to flat sequential pipelines and tie revision triggers to verification failures. Proposed follow-ups: verifier-fired vs manager-judged loops, and activation probes to separate deference from calibrated uncertainty. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14767v2)
  - Huang et al. (Google DeepMind / UIUC, ICLR 2024): without external feedback LLMs struggle to self-correct reasoning and sometimes get worse. Multi-agent debate does not beat self-consistency at an equal number of responses. [secondary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)
  - Kamoi et al., TACL 2024: no prior work shows successful self-correction from prompted-LLM feedback outside tasks exceptionally suited to it. Self-correction works with reliable external feedback, and large-scale fine-tuning can enable it. [secondary] (https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78/)
  - CRITIC (Gou et al., ICLR 2024): LLMs improve outputs when critique comes from tool interaction (search engines for fact checks, code interpreters for debugging) across QA, code synthesis and toxicity reduction. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738)
  - Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models": responses that match a user's views are more likely to be preferred, and optimising against preference models sometimes trades accuracy for agreement. Tomek Korbak is a co-author. [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week: Audit every "review and revise" loop in his pipelines, Morning Signal included. For each loop, write down what the reviewer can actually *verify*: URL resolves, number appears verbatim in the source, required section present, tests pass, schema valid. Replace taste-based "make it better" loop-backs with deterministic checks that fire a targeted revision only on a specific failure, and pass the failing evidence along, not an opinion. Keep any subjective review as a single non-binding pass, or as a note to the human. Then run his own small paired test: 10 inputs, flat vs loop-back, and compare hedge counts per 1,000 words, tokens and a blind side-by-side read.
