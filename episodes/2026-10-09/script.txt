GEORGE: And it fits a prediction doing the rounds. Ingar Haaland, via Marginal Revolution, argued that work which can be fully outsourced to AI, like simple survey experiments, is in for a big shock.

GEORGE: Minimum three gates. One, a demand-slope test, raise the price and confirm yes-votes fall monotonically. MIA: And that "coherent is not correct" line is the perfect bridge to today's deep dive, because it's the same disease in a different system. We trust a process because it looks rigorous, not because it's verified.
GEORGE: The manager-agent paper.
MIA: From LIACS, Leiden University. Burak Agachan, Max van Duijn and Amirhossein Zohrehvand. Revised on the seventh of October. And it goes straight at the most common multi-agent pattern there is: the manager who reviews a worker's output and can send it back for a revision.
GEORGE: Which almost every framework gives you by default. The manager agent has loop-back authority. It reads the state and can issue a binding "fix this."
MIA: And the result is blunt. Letting the manager send work back made the reports worse, cost fifty-one and a half percent more tokens, and the damage traced entirely to the revision step. Not to the hierarchy. To the act of revising.
GEORGE: Let me set up the tension first, because the paper frames it beautifully. Organisation theory says authority is good. A manager with the power to send work back forces convergence and polish. That's why human orgs have managers.
MIA: And the LLM literature predicts the opposite.
GEORGE: Precisely the opposite. A worker trained with RLHF responds to authority with compliance, not with belief revision. So the paper's framing is that an authority link isn't just a control channel, it's a conditioning signal on the worker's next generation. You tell the model an authority rejected its work, and that reshapes what it writes next, regardless of whether the work was wrong.
MIA: Tell me the experiment, because the design is tight.
GEORGE: Five roles. A Researcher with ChromaDB retrieval, an Analyst, a Writer, a Critic with tool-grounded checks, and a Manager. They write business-intelligence reports on forty-three gaming laptops, from specs and reviews. Each product is run twice. Once flat, where the Manager is just a non-binding peer router. Once hierarchical, where the Manager issues binding directives, capped at two revision loops.
MIA: So eighty-six runs, same task, same inputs, only the coordination structure changes. That's the clean paired design.
GEORGE: And models are randomly assigned to roles from a pool, GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Qwen-3.5-122B, GLM-5 and Mistral-Large-3. The task is deliberately half-verifiable. The specs can be checked against listings. The analysis cannot.
MIA: And flat wins.
GEORGE: Flat wins on Utility, four-point-seven-one-five versus four-point-six-two-one, effect size zero-point-four-two, p equals zero-point-zero-zero-nine. MIA: So the manager didn't make it more accurate. It made it more hedged and more expensive.
GEORGE: Fifty-three percent more hedging words per thousand words, five-point-oh-three versus three-point-three-oh, at the same length. Fifty-one and a half percent more tokens, forty percent higher cost, and a third more wall-clock time.
MIA: Now here's what makes the paper more than a benchmark. They locate the mechanism. It isn't the hierarchy. It's the revision.
GEORGE: This is the key result. The first hierarchical draft is indistinguishable from a flat report. Same hedging, can't tell them apart. The gap only opens after the first revision. The second draft adds nearly two hedges per thousand words while keeping eighty-eight percent of the original text word-for-word.
MIA: So the worker isn't rethinking. It's pattern-matching to "I got sent back, so I'd better soften everything and pad it."
GEORGE: It complies by hedging and padding, not by reconsidering. And clarity falls with every loop, about zero-point-one-four points per loop in their regression. The tell-tale detail: in runs where the Manager had authority but never actually used it, there was no harm at all.
MIA: Which rules out the "maybe hierarchy makes the model nervous from the start" story. The damage is dose-dependent on actual revisions.
GEORGE: And it lines up with older work. Huang and colleagues at DeepMind showed that without external feedback, models struggle to self-correct reasoning and sometimes get worse. Sharma and co-authors on sycophancy showed preference-optimised models drift toward telling the evaluator what it wants to hear. This is both of those, in a multi-agent costume.
MIA: So it's not that review is useless. It's that this particular review has nothing to push against. The manager can only opine. "Make it better." And the worker's only lever in response is tone.
GEORGE: That's the thesis sentence of the whole paper, actually. And they phrase it as a design rule: a Manager with loop-back authority earns its cost when it can verify something, and becomes a liability when it can only opine.
MIA: Verify, don't opine. And the companion result is when loops do help. Because they do, sometimes.
GEORGE: The Kamoi survey in TACL found self-correction works when there's reliable external feedback. And CRITIC, Gou and colleagues, showed real gains when the critique comes from a tool. A search engine checking a fact. A code interpreter catching a bug. The common thread is a ground truth outside the model's own opinion.
MIA: So the line between verify and opine is really the line between "a tool said this claim is false" and "the manager feels the analysis is thin." The first is a verifier. The second is taste.
GEORGE: And that's the hard case for you and me, isn't it? An LLM fact-checker with a search tool is clearly a verifier. But an LLM judge scoring "strategic depth" one to five, is that a verifier or just a manager with opinions in a lab coat?
MIA: It's a manager with opinions. And the paper's own numbers kind of admit that about judging in general.
GEORGE: They're honest about the limits. Judge agreement is low, Krippendorff's alpha down at zero-point-zero-eight to zero-point-one-seven for clarity. The blind human check, twenty pairs, two raters, points the same direction but isn't significant, flat wins fifty-four percent. And the number of loops is chosen by the Manager itself, so the dose-response is correlational, not assigned.
MIA: So which result do we actually lean on?
GEORGE: Utility is the robust one. All five judges favour flat, and it survives dropping any single judge. Clarity is the fragile one, it leans on a shaky hedge lexicon that can't tell calibrated uncertainty from evasion. So: trust "flat produced more useful reports." Hold "flat produced clearer reports" more loosely.
MIA: And it's one short synthesis task, one model generation, one Manager prompt, forty-three pairs. This is a signal, not a law.
GEORGE: Which is exactly why the recommendation is a design default rather than a universal truth. Default to flat sequential pipelines, and tie revision triggers to verification failures, not to a manager's judgement.
MIA: This is the part that hits home, because I've lived it. I had a reviewer agent on my script writer, and every pass made it more "balanced." More "could," more "may," less spine. I thought I was imagining it. Turns out it's a measurable effect with an effect size.
GEORGE: You were watching the hedging climb in real time.
MIA: So let me make this concrete for anyone with a pipeline, because this is a this-week job, not a someday job. Go audit every "review and revise" loop you have. Is the schema valid?
GEORGE: Those are all real verifiers. Deterministic, external, pass or fail.
MIA: Right. And then replace every taste-based "make it better" loop-back with a check that fires a targeted revision only on a specific failure. GEORGE: Because that gives the worker something to push against instead of a vibe to appease. You're converting the manager from a critic into a test harness.
MIA: And any genuinely subjective review, keep it as a single non-binding pass. Or better, route it to the human. Don't let taste loop.
GEORGE: One non-binding pass can't compound. It's the loop that compounds the hedging, each turn keeping most of the previous turn and sanding off a little more conviction.
