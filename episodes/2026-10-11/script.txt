MIA: Morning Lucas. It's Sunday, so we've got room for one big story, and it sits right in your field. For the first time in more than a decade, global call-centre employment is shrinking.
GEORGE: And the headline number isn't really why it's worth a whole episode. Customer service is the one occupation where we have causal micro evidence on generative AI, near-real-time payroll data, and some well-known null results, all about the same jobs.
MIA: So it's the cleanest test case for the question everyone hand-waves about: how does a productivity gain you can actually measure turn into fewer jobs. Or not.
GEORGE: Let's start with the signal. Revelio Labs put out new global headcount data on the sixth of October, and Tyler Cowen flagged it on Marginal Revolution a few days later. Global call-centre headcount is now five point three percent below its peak in December twenty twenty-three.
MIA: And that's not one bad quarter.
GEORGE: No. Year-on-year declines for eight straight quarters, after more than a decade of growth.
MIA: What caught my eye was the geography. It didn't fall everywhere at once.
GEORGE: There's a clear sequence. Between twenty twenty and twenty twenty-four, lower-income countries drove the growth while high-income markets were already declining. Global growth turned negative in mid twenty twenty-four, and by the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-five all markets were declining. China and Nigeria are the ones they name as still growing.
MIA: Which fits the pay gap. Average hourly pay in call centres is twenty-two dollars fifty-two in high-income countries, five forty-four in upper-middle-income, and two thirty-two in lower-middle-income. You'd expect software to bite first where labour costs twenty-two dollars an hour.
GEORGE: Plausible, though that's your reading, not Revelio's. What they do show is a neat comparison in twelve middle-income countries. Before ChatGPT launched in November twenty twenty-two, call-centre headcount there roughly kept pace with other office and administrative roles. Afterwards, call centres show sluggish growth or outright declines, while the rest of white-collar work looks fairly robust.
MIA: So a sort of informal control group. Same countries, similar jobs, different trajectory.
GEORGE: Informal is the right word. But the part I'd put at the centre isn't the level, it's the flows. Taking November twenty twenty-two as the baseline, the entry rate fell from twenty-five point one to seventeen point two per hundred workers. The exit rate fell too, from twenty-two point three to eighteen point five.
MIA: So both fell. People leave less, and firms hire a lot less.
GEORGE: Exactly, entries fell faster than exits. So the sector isn't shrinking because people are being laid off. It's shrinking because firms hire fewer people.
MIA: And in call centres that's a massive lever, because churn is brutal. The Brynjolfsson paper we'll get to cites industry estimates that about sixty percent of contact-centre agents leave every year, at a cost of ten to twenty thousand dollars per agent.
GEORGE: Sixty percent. Think of a bathtub with the plug half out. You don't need to bail water out to lower the level. You just turn the tap down a bit, and within a year the level has dropped.
MIA: Which also means there's never a headline. No mass layoff. Just job ads that don't get posted.
GEORGE: And before we build on it, Revelio's own caveats. They say the decline "is surely multifaceted". Part of it could be a correction from the post-COVID hiring boom, part could be cost-cutting driven by higher interest rates. And they can't see which firms actually replaced people with chatbots.
MIA: Fair. So the data say: the sector shrinks, it shrinks through hiring, and the timing lines up with ChatGPT. They don't say AI did it.
GEORGE: Right. Which is why we need a mechanism. Why would AI specifically cut hiring, rather than cause layoffs?
MIA: Which brings us to the paper everybody cites. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work".
GEORGE: The setup is why people trust it. Three million chats, by five thousand one hundred and seventy-two agents, at a Fortune five hundred business-software firm and its subcontractors. Eighty-nine percent of the agents are outside the US, mainly in the Philippines. Chats average about forty minutes, so these are proper technical support conversations.
MIA: And the tool was a GPT-based assistant suggesting responses to the agent. Not talking to customers directly.
GEORGE: Correct, it was designed to augment, not replace. The identification is the clever part. The rollout was staggered over late twenty twenty and early twenty twenty-one, driven by boring constraints: bottlenecks in training sessions and a licence budget. Managers spread their team members across sessions, so within one team, some people had the tool and some didn't yet.
MIA: So you get variation under the same manager, same queue, same customers. That kills a lot of the obvious confounders.
GEORGE: Exactly. Difference-in-differences with agent and tenure fixed effects, robust to the newer staggered-adoption estimators and to an instrument based on the team's adoption date. And a small randomised pilot, about fifty workers with twenty-two treated, gives similar results.
MIA: Okay, the numbers.
GEORGE: Resolutions per hour go up by zero point three, from a pre-treatment mean of one point nine seven. That's fifteen point two percent. Handle time drops three point seven minutes, about eight and a half percent. The resolution rate rises one point three percentage points, but that's not significant, and there's no economically significant change in customer satisfaction.
MIA: And the distribution is the real story, right?
GEORGE: It is. The gains go overwhelmingly to novices and low-skill agents, about thirty percent for the less-skilled and less-experienced, thirty-six for the lowest skill quintile. Agents who had the tool from the start reach about two and a half resolutions per hour after two months. Agents who never got it need eight to ten months to get there.
MIA: So two months with the tool is worth more than six months without.
GEORGE: That's their finding. And for the most skilled agents, no productivity gain at all, plus small but statistically significant declines in resolution rate and customer satisfaction.
MIA: Why would the best people get worse?
GEORGE: Look at how it was built. The model was fine-tuned on conversations labelled with outcomes, with top performers' chats flagged. So the suggestions encode what the best agents do. The tacit stuff: how they phrase a fix, when they ask a clarifying question, how they calm someone down. For a novice, that's a stream of expert advice. For the expert, it's their own behaviour handed back, slightly averaged, and possibly a distraction.
MIA: So the tool bottles the top performer and pours it out for the juniors.
GEORGE: Nicely put. There were side effects too. Customers became more polite, asked for a manager less often, and attrition fell among newer workers.
MIA: But here's the so what for me. What's the scarce input this replaces? It isn't the agent. It's the six months of on-the-job learning.
GEORGE: Exactly that. Firms used to hire juniors and tolerate them being slow because they would get good. AI flattens that experience curve. It substitutes for the very learning period that made a junior worth hiring.
MIA: And the authors flag an incentive problem I think is underrated.
GEORGE: Yes. Top agents' conversations are the training data. If they contribute less original input, future models have less good material to learn from. The model could degrade precisely because it worked.
MIA: Hold that thought, I see exactly that in my own tools. But first: we have a fifteen percent productivity gain. Why would that mean fewer jobs?
GEORGE: It doesn't have to, and that's the crucial step. A fifteen percent gain only becomes fewer jobs if the firm doesn't serve proportionally more customers. The Canaries authors say it explicitly: higher productivity can raise or lower employment, depending on how elastic demand is for those workers' output.
MIA: And support is the classic cost centre. No board meeting ever said "great news, we can now handle fifteen percent more support conversations".
GEORGE: So demand is inelastic. Same number of tickets, each handled faster, you need fewer agents. Then combine that with sixty percent annual turnover —
MIA: — and you don't fire anyone, you just don't replace the people who leave. The Revelio pattern exactly.
GEORGE: And the people who don't get hired are entry-level, because that's precisely the input the AI made less valuable.
MIA: I've lived a tiny version of this. I built a support bot for a webshop. Ticket volume handled by humans dropped by a third, and nobody was fired. Two people left in spring and they just weren't replaced.
GEORGE: That's the Revelio story at the scale of one firm.
MIA: But would any statistic ever show it? No layoff, no unemployment claim. Just two vacancies that never existed.
GEORGE: A headcount snapshot would barely notice. A survey of the remaining staff would show them doing fine. The only place it shows up is in hiring flows. And that's the methods point running through this whole episode: which margin you measure largely decides what you find.
MIA: To be fair to Brynjolfsson and colleagues, they never claim the jobs part.
GEORGE: No, they write that the paper "is not designed to shed light on the aggregate employment or wage effects". They can't observe wages, overall labour demand or the skill composition of hiring. One firm, causal on productivity, silent on jobs.
MIA: Which is where the Canaries paper comes in. Brynjolfsson again, with Chandar and Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?", updated in August.
GEORGE: It uses payroll data from ADP, which pays more than twenty-six million US workers. A balanced panel of firms with three and a half to five million employees per month, from January twenty twenty-one to June twenty twenty-six.
MIA: And exposure measured how?
GEORGE: Two ways. Ratings of what GPT-four could do in each occupation, and the Anthropic Economic Index, which matters here because it separates usage that automates a task from usage that complements the worker.
MIA: Headline number.
GEORGE: Workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five in AI-exposed occupations are now nineteen percent below where they'd be if they had kept pace with less-exposed peers. With the July twenty twenty-five data, that was fifteen percent. So the gap is widening.
MIA: In plain levels?
GEORGE: Twenty-two to twenty-five year olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about eleven percent between November twenty twenty-two and June twenty twenty-six. The same age group in the three least exposed quintiles grew about ten percent.
MIA: And experienced workers in the same jobs?
GEORGE: No comparable gap. And the rest lines up almost point for point with what we just said. The adjustment runs through reduced hiring, not more separations. It's concentrated where AI usage automates. Where usage is complementary, employment is flat or rising. And it shows up in employment, not in base pay.
MIA: So wages don't move, headcount does. Look only at earnings and you'd conclude nothing happened.
GEORGE: And their dashboard puts customer service representatives in the highest exposure group, saying they "see similar trends to software developers: noticeable declines for the first two age groups, with expansion for the remaining age groups."
MIA: Expansion for the older groups. That's the experience curve showing up in the data. Seniors still needed, juniors not hired.
GEORGE: Now let me be the precise one. Earlier versions headlined regression estimates with firm-level controls: thirteen percent with the July twenty twenty-five data, sixteen with September. Now they emphasise the simpler descriptive gap.
MIA: That's a bit of a yellow flag for me. Switching your headline to the raw gap when the raw gap is bigger.
GEORGE: I'd read it more charitably, because they're unusually candid. They call these descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates, and they list the problems themselves. Some divergent trends predate ChatGPT, around COVID. The estimates attenuate when you control for occupational education. The pattern is more pronounced in ADP than in national survey benchmarks. And the ADP sample over-represents larger firms and more AI-exposed occupations.
MIA: The education one is interesting. Confounder, or mechanism?
GEORGE: That's the open question, and they say they can't tell yet. Education might be a confounder, or it might be the very channel AI works through, because it goes with codified knowledge. Control for it and you might be controlling away the thing you want to measure.
MIA: And for the economy as a whole?
GEORGE: Overall employment in the ADP sample rose about six percent from November twenty twenty-two to June twenty twenty-six. Even the most exposed quintile grew about four percent. Hence their line: "no widespread, economy-wide job displacement".
MIA: Okay, now the awkward part. Read only those two papers and you'd think it's settled. It isn't.
GEORGE: Denmark first. Humlum and Vestergaard, an NBER working paper revised in March, linking Danish adoption surveys to administrative records. Most employers in exposed occupations have adopted chatbot initiatives, and workers report productivity gains. But difference-in-differences gives precise nulls on earnings and recorded hours, at worker and workplace level. They rule out effects larger than two percent within two years of ChatGPT.
MIA: And customer support is in their sample?
GEORGE: According to a presentation of their work, their eleven occupations include customer support specialists. What they do find is reorganised work, with new tasks like content generation, AI oversight and integration.
MIA: Then the executives, my favourite of the lot. Baslandze, Edwards, Graham and coauthors, NBER, March. Nearly seven hundred and fifty senior financial executives, mostly from the CFO Survey at the end of twenty twenty-five.

GEORGE: Two rules, both live since this summer. First, the AI Act, Article fifty, paragraph one. Fines can reach fifteen million euros or three percent of global annual turnover.
MIA: The second rule is the one I find more interesting for the Netherlands.
GEORGE: The EU directive on distance financial-services contracts, applying from the nineteenth of June. MIA: So if you sell insurance or credit online here and there's a chatbot in the flow, the customer can ask for a human, in their language.
