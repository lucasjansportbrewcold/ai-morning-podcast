## Deep dive: Call centres as AI's first labour-market canary: what the evidence shows, and what it doesn't

- Why this topic, why now:
  - Revelio Labs published new global headcount data on 6 October 2026 showing that call-centre employment is shrinking for the first time in more than a decade. Tyler Cowen picked it up on 10 October. Customer service is the occupation where we have the best micro evidence on generative AI (the Brynjolfsson/Li/Raymond study), the best near-real-time payroll evidence (the "Canaries" paper, updated August 2026) and well-known null results (Denmark). So it is the cleanest case for asking how a measurable productivity gain turns, or doesn't turn, into fewer jobs.
  - For Lucas this is the economics of AI in his own field. It is also a methods lesson: which margin you measure (the stock of workers, earnings, or hiring flows) largely decides what you find. It also has a direct Dutch angle, because two EU rules that apply since summer 2026 shape how far customer contact can be automated.
  - Not covered before: past deep dives were on agent security, replication, evals and judges. The 10-09 episode covered AI price declines (Epoch), not labour effects.

- Outline:
  1. **The signal: a decline through hiring, not firing.** Revelio's global data show call-centre headcount 5.3% below its December 2023 peak, with year-on-year declines for eight straight quarters. The decline started in high-income countries, then reached middle-income countries, and by Q4 2025 all markets were declining. The mechanism matters more than the level: since November 2022 the entry rate fell from 25.1 to 17.2 per 100 workers, while the exit rate fell from 22.3 to 18.5. Entries fell faster than exits, so the sector is shrinking because it hires fewer people, not because it lays more off. In an industry with very high churn, a firm can shrink a lot simply by not replacing people who leave.
  2. **The micro mechanism: AI bottles the top performer.** Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied a GPT-based assistant that was rolled out in stages to about 5,000 support agents. The tool was fine-tuned on conversations, with top performers' chats flagged, so its suggestions encode the tacit behaviour of the best agents. The gains go overwhelmingly to novices and low-skill agents: agents treated from the start reach after two months the productivity that untreated agents reach after more than six. The top performers gain nothing, and their quality falls slightly. The key point is that AI here flattens the experience curve. The scarce input it substitutes for is the months of on-the-job learning that made a junior worth hiring.
  3. **From productivity to headcount: it depends on demand elasticity.** A 15% productivity gain only becomes fewer jobs if the firm doesn't serve proportionally more customers. The Canaries authors say this explicitly: higher productivity can raise or lower employment, depending on how elastic demand is for those workers' output. Customer support is close to a cost centre: few firms want 15% more support conversations. Combine that with high turnover and the predicted adjustment is exactly what Revelio sees: fewer new hires, especially at the entry level, rather than layoffs. The Brynjolfsson et al. paper itself warns that it cannot observe wages, overall labour demand or hiring composition.
  4. **The US payroll evidence: young workers absorb it.** The August 2026 update of "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" uses ADP payroll data through June 2026. Workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations are now 19% below where they would be if they had kept pace with less-exposed peers, up from 15% in the July 2025 data. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. The gap arises mainly from reduced hiring, not separations. It is concentrated in occupations where AI usage automates rather than complements, and it shows up in employment rather than base pay. Customer service representatives are in the highest exposure group and show declines for the two youngest age groups, with expansion for older groups. The authors are unusually candid that this is descriptive, not causal (see the skeptic points below).
  5. **The counter-evidence, and how to reconcile it.** Humlum and Vestergaard (Denmark, linked survey and administrative data) find precise null effects on earnings and hours, ruling out effects larger than 2% within two years of ChatGPT. A CFO survey of nearly 750 executives finds AI-driven US employment expected to fall less than 0.4% in 2026. Yet the same survey's "Negative Exposure Index" puts Office and Administrative Support, which includes customer service representatives, at 2.025. That is the only occupation group listed where executives mention replacement more often than enhancement. A CPS-based synthetic DiD (Chen et al., Chicago) even finds higher earnings and unchanged unemployment in exposed occupations. The reconciliation: the null results measure incumbents (their earnings, hours and unemployment), while the negative results measure inflows of young workers. If firms adjust by not hiring, incumbents look fine and the effect only appears in entry rates and young cohorts. These studies can all be right at the same time.
  6. **Where displaced entrants go, and the Dutch/EU design constraint.** Revelio: of call-centre workers who found a new job within 12 months, 49.7% moved sideways into customer service, sales or office/HR roles. Only 10.8% reached technical support, customer success or software/data, where median pay is 7% to 13% higher. In the EU, full automation is not the legal default anyway. AI Act Article 50(1) requires that people be told at first contact that they are dealing with AI (applies from 2 August 2026). For distance financial-services contracts, Dutch law now gives consumers a right to human intervention when chatbots or other online tools are used (in force 19 June 2026). The design that fits both the economics and the law is AI-first contact with a well-built human escalation path. That makes the escalation layer, not the bot, the interesting thing to build or audit.

- Facts and examples with sources:
  - **Revelio Labs, "After a decade of growth, global call center employment is shrinking"** (Caelan Wilkie-Rogers, 6 October 2026): https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/ai-and-work/after-a-decade-of-growth-global-call-center-employment-is-shrinking
    - Global call-centre headcount is 5.3% below its December 2023 peak; year-on-year declines for eight straight quarters, ending more than a decade of growth. [primary]
    - Sequence: in 2020–2024, lower-income countries drove growth while high-income markets declined; global growth turned negative in mid-2024; by Q4 2025 all markets were declining. China and Nigeria are named as places that kept growing. [primary]
    - In 12 middle-income countries, call-centre headcount generally kept pace with other office and administrative roles before ChatGPT's November 2022 launch. Afterwards, call centres show sluggish growth or outright declines while the rest of white-collar work looks fairly robust. [primary]
    - Entry rate fell from 25.1 to 17.2 per 100 workers and exit rate from 22.3 to 18.5 (November 2022 baseline); entries fell faster than exits. [primary]
    - Average hourly pay in call centres: $22.52 in high-income, $5.44 in upper-middle-income and $2.32 in lower-middle-income countries. [primary]
    - Growth over the last year in the 12 middle-income countries: software and data +8.9% (more than twice call-centre hourly pay); customer success and client relations +7.2% (50% pay premium); technical support +2.6% (31% pay premium). [primary]
    - Of call-centre workers re-employed within 12 months, 49.7% moved to customer service, sales or office/HR roles; 10.8% reached technical support, customer success or software/data. Median pay changes for same-country movers at unchanged seniority: technical support +11.4%, customer success +7.1%, software and data +12.7%. [primary]
    - Revelio's own caveats: the decline "is surely multifaceted" (it could partly be a correction from the post-COVID hiring boom, or cost-cutting driven by higher interest rates), and Revelio cannot observe which firms replace people with chatbots. [primary]
    - Tyler Cowen highlighted the piece on Marginal Revolution on 10 October 2026: https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/10/earth-facts-of-the-day.html [primary]
  - **Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work"** (arXiv v2, 7 November 2024; first draft April 2023): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.11771
    - Data: 3 million chats by 5,172 agents at a Fortune 500 business-software firm and its subcontractors; 89% of agents are outside the US, mainly in the Philippines; chats average about 40 minutes. [primary]
    - Identification: a staggered rollout in late 2020 and early 2021, driven by training-session bottlenecks and a licence budget. Managers spread team members across sessions, so adoption varied within teams. Estimated by difference-in-differences with agent and tenure fixed effects; robust to Sun-Abraham, Callaway-Sant'Anna and other estimators, and to an instrument based on team adoption date. A small randomised pilot of about 50 workers (22 treated) gives similar results. [primary]
    - Main effect: resolutions per hour +0.30, or 15.2%, from a pre-treatment mean of 1.97. Handle time falls 3.7 minutes (8.5%), chats per hour +15%, resolution rate +1.3 pp (not significant), and no economically significant change in customer satisfaction (NPS). [primary]
    - Versions disagree slightly: the 2023 NBER Digest reported 13.8% and 35% for less experienced and lower-skill workers; v2 reports 15% on average, about 30% for less-skilled/less-experienced workers and 36% for the lowest skill quintile. [primary] (https://www.nber.org/digest/20236/measuring-productivity-impact-generative-ai)
    - Agents treated from the start reach about 2.5 resolutions per hour after two months; never-treated agents need 8 to 10 months to get there. Treated agents with two months of tenure perform as well as untreated agents with more than six months. [primary]
    - For the most skilled agents: no productivity gain, plus small but statistically significant declines in resolution rate and customer satisfaction. The authors flag an incentive problem: top agents' conversations are the training data, so less original input from them could degrade future models. [primary]
    - The tool was fine-tuned on conversations labelled with outcomes, with top performers flagged, and was designed to augment agents rather than replace them. Customers became more polite, asked for a manager less often, and attrition fell among newer workers. [primary]
    - Industry estimates cited in the paper: about 60% of contact-centre agents leave each year, at a cost of $10,000 to $20,000 per agent. [primary, as cited by the paper]
    - The authors state the paper "is not designed to shed light on the aggregate employment or wage effects" and that they cannot observe wages, overall labour demand or the skill composition of hiring. [primary]
  - **Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts…"** (August 2026 version, ADP data through June 2026): https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf
    - Sample: a balanced panel of firms from ADP (which pays more than 26 million US workers), with 3.5 to 5 million employees per month, January 2021 to June 2026; full-time workers under 70. Exposure measures: Eloundou et al. GPT-4 β ratings and the Anthropic Economic Index (automation vs complementary usage). [primary]
    - Workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations are 19% below the kept-pace counterfactual relative to less-exposed peers, up from 15% at the July 2025 data vintage. In levels, 22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11% between November 2022 and June 2026, while the same age group in the three least exposed quintiles grew about 10%. [primary]
    - Earlier versions headlined regression estimates with firm-level controls (13% at July 2025 data, 16% at September 2025 data); the authors now emphasise the simpler descriptive gap. [primary]
    - Adjustment runs through reduced hiring, not more separations; it is concentrated where AI usage automates; employment is flat or rising where usage is complementary; base pay does not show the adjustment. [primary]
    - Overall employment in the ADP sample rose about 6% from November 2022 to June 2026; the most exposed quintile grew about 4%. Hence "no widespread, economy-wide job displacement". [primary]
    - Caveats the authors themselves raise: some divergent trends predate ChatGPT (around COVID); estimates attenuate when controlling for occupational education; the pattern is more pronounced in ADP than in national survey benchmarks; the ADP sample over-represents larger firms and more AI-exposed occupations.     - The Stanford dashboard says customer service representatives are in the highest exposure group and "see similar trends to software developers: noticeable declines for the first two age groups, with expansion for the remaining age groups."     - Danish adoption surveys linked to administrative records. Most employers in exposed occupations have adopted chatbot initiatives and workers report productivity gains. - Skeptic angle / open questions:
  - None of the headline labour findings is causal. Revelio and Canaries are descriptive; the Brynjolfsson et al. study is causal but within one firm, on productivity, not employment. The post-COVID hiring boom and higher interest rates are both plausible co-drivers, and both Revelio and the Canaries authors say so.
  - The Canaries gap shrinks when controlling for education and is larger in ADP than in national surveys. Education may be a confounder, or it may be the very channel through which AI operates (codified knowledge). The authors say they cannot yet tell which.
  - Exposure indices built from Claude or GPT-4 usage measure what AI *could* touch, not what firms actually deployed. Chen et al. explicitly call their estimate an "intent-to-treat" on exposure.
  - The 2021–2023 tool in Brynjolfsson et al. augmented agents. Today's deployments increasingly answer customers directly, which is a different treatment, and no comparable causal study of full-automation deployments appears in the sources used here.
  - What to watch: monthly updates of the Canaries dashboard; whether Revelio's middle-income declines continue in 2027; the first Dutch enforcement of Article 50 and of the right to human intervention.

- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week:
  - **Research:** Sketch a Dutch version of the Revelio/Canaries test. Use CBS microdata (remote access) on jobs in SBI 82.20 and in customer-contact occupations. Measure *entry rates by age cohort* before and after November 2022, compared with other office and administrative work. Measure flows, not stocks: if adjustment runs through hiring, a stock or earnings regression will show the Danish-style null even when entry is collapsing. Pre-register the comparison groups and a pre-trend check (the Canaries pre-COVID divergence is the warning).
  - **Business:** Build an Article 50 plus human-intervention audit for Dutch SME chatbots, especially for anyone selling financial products at a distance. Check that the AI disclosure appears at first contact, that a free human escalation in the same language exists, and that it is logged. Treat the escalation path as the product: route by a calibrated confidence gate, and hand the human a summary plus the transcript.
  - **Own agents:** The "AI bottles the top performer" mechanism applies to Lucas's own skills and RAG tools. They encode whoever wrote the examples, and they help the novice user most. Keep a feed of expert-corrected outputs flowing back into the examples. Otherwise you get the paper's incentive problem, where experts stop contributing original work and the model stops improving.
