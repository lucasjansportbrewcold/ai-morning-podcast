## News
### 1. Effort is the new prompt: Anthropic tells users to drop "think hard" and start Opus 5.5 at Medium
- Why it matters for Lucas: His Claude skills and agent prompts probably still contain "think carefully"-style scaffolding and default effort settings from older models. Anthropic now says that wastes tokens. The effort setting has become a cost/quality control to tune per task, and his agents should treat it that way.
- Facts:
  - Anthropic published a guide on 12 November 2026 on using Claude Opus 5.5 as a "daily driver" for knowledge work [primary] (https://claude.com/resources/articles/using-claude-opus-5-5-as-your-daily-driver-for-knowledge-work)
  - Advice: start at Medium effort (the default). On Anthropic's coding and knowledge-work evals, Opus 5.5 at Medium matched or beat Opus 5 at High. Move to High only when Medium stalls. "Higher settings think longer and cost more without always producing better work." [primary] (same URL)
  - "Thinking is always on for Opus 5.5, and it decides how much to think based on your request", so phrases like "think hard" should be deleted [primary] (same URL)
  - Other advice: say what "done" looks like in the first message, ask for finished files rather than outlines, attach charts instead of retyping data, and name the styles you don't want rather than giving vague design instructions [primary] (same URL)
  - Numbers in the guide: Opus 5.5 costs $4/$20 per million input/output tokens. In one demo it finished a financial analysis in 63 minutes versus 93 for Opus 5, "at half the cost". Early testers saw "20 to 25% fewer output tokens" at Medium [primary] (same URL)
  - The Decoder reports that Opus 5.5 is now the default model on all paid plans [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-says-outdated-prompting-habits-are-slowing-down-claude-opus-5-5/)
  - Related: Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 were released on 9 November 2026 with five effort levels (Low, Medium, High, Xhigh, Max). Price: $8/$40 per million input/output tokens, or $16/$80 in fast mode. Anthropic says Fable 5.2 at High matches Opus 5.5's FrontierCode 2.0 score "for roughly half the cost per task", even though its per-token price is twice as high. Fable 5.2 scores 49.1% on FrontierCode 2.0 at Xhigh [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The "Medium matches old High" claim comes from Anthropic's own evals. The Fable-vs-Opus cost comparison also shows that per-token price no longer predicts per-task cost: token efficiency dominates. The only reliable answer comes from measuring cost per completed task on your own workload. Also open: do prompt habits that helped older models now actively hurt, or are they just wasteful? The guide says "slowing down", not "degrading".
- Mia hook: "I grepped my skills folder for 'think step by step' and found it in eleven files. I removed it and ran my eval set at Medium versus High. What would you expect to happen to the pass rate and to the bill? And should an agent pick its own effort level per subtask?"

### 2. Two open-weights giants for agents: Meituan's LongCat-2.5 (MIT, trained without Nvidia) and Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Small
- Why it matters for Lucas: Frontier-class agentic models with permissive licences keep arriving, which matters for EU data-sovereignty use cases and for cheap API alternatives in his pipelines. Running them locally is out of reach, though: these are hundreds of billions of parameters even when only a fraction is active.
- Facts:
  - LongCat-2.5 is a native multimodal mixture-of-experts model with 1.6 trillion total parameters and about 48 billion active per token, released under the MIT licence [primary] (https://huggingface.co/meituan-longcat/LongCat-2.5)
  - Scores from the model card: Terminal Bench v2.1 88.4, DeepSWE v1.1 64.6, BrowseComp 91.9, SWE Atlas Codebase QnA 58.3, MathVision (with tools) 96.5 [primary] (same URL)
  - It uses W4A4 quantization (4-bit expert weights and activations, native INT4), with 43.3% higher prefill throughput than a W8A8 baseline, and is optimised for domestic (Chinese) accelerators [primary] (same URL)
  - The Decoder reports that the sample deployment runs on 194 Huawei Ascend chips with no mention of Nvidia, a 1M-token input context, and API pricing of about €0.27/€1.07 per million input/output tokens [secondary] (https://the-decoder.com/meituan-releases-a-1-6-trillion-parameter-agent-model-that-never-touched-an-nvidia-gpu/)
  - Sources disagree on the comparison: The Decoder's teaser says the Terminal Bench score lands "just behind Claude Opus 5", while its own comparison table puts DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ahead at 90.6 (and at 74.2 on DeepSWE) [secondary] (same URL)
  - Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Small (announced 5 November 2026): 594B total / 17B active MoE, 256K context, Apache 2.0, with a "variable effort" setting. Reported scores: SWE-Bench Pro v2 (Hard) 86.3%, DeepSWE v1.1 72.1%. Trained on Nvidia GB300 NVL72 racks in NVFP4 [primary] (https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/)
  - Thinking Machines' page says the weights were first closed and served on Tinker, with the full Apache 2.0 weights to follow. A weekly newsletter reports the open-weights release on 12 November [secondary] (https://mindandmachineweekly.substack.com/p/mind-and-machine-weekly-15-november)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: All benchmark numbers are self-reported. "Trained entirely on Chinese hardware" is an inference from Meituan's silence about Nvidia, not a statement in the model card. Watch how US remote-access export rules affect Chinese labs' compute, and whether European providers host these models under EU data residency.
- Mia hook: "Variable effort shows up in open weights too now. Would you route the cheap bulk work of a RAG pipeline (extraction, reranking) to an MIT-licensed model on an EU host, and keep Claude only for the hard steps? How would you prove the quality is good enough?"

### 3. NBER: Generative AI built into a Chinese e-commerce platform raised small sellers' sales by 51.6%
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is firm-level evidence, not task-level evidence. AI delivered as shared infrastructure, inside the software firms already use, gave the biggest gains to the smallest firms. That is directly relevant to whether Dutch SMEs gain more from embedded AI than from DIY tools, which could be a business angle.
- Facts:
  - NBER Working Paper 36003 (November 2026) by Panle Jia Barwick, Lu Fang, Jinhan Liu, Huilan Xu and Zhe Yuan studies a leading Chinese e-commerce platform that in 2024 built a generative AI system into the operating software firms use to run their businesses [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w36003)
  - Design: complete records of each firm's AI use linked to monthly operations and sales. Matched difference-in-differences on firms with annual sales below RMB 5 million, with instrumental-variable estimates as a robustness check [primary] (same URL)
  - Result: adoption raises monthly sales by 51.6%, mainly through a 47.0% increase in the number of orders. Adopters reach more customers, update product lines faster and deliver better quality, and the smallest firms gain the most in proportional terms [primary] (same URL)
  - The authors identify two mechanisms. First, AI lowers the cost of routine work: more product content and listing changes with less staff time per order, with the largest gains for firms that had the highest pre-adoption costs relative to sales. Second, AI reduces information frictions, with the largest gains where demand is hard to predict and matching quality is poor [primary] (same URL)
  - At market level: categories with more pre-existing information friction see more sales by existing firms, more entry and more new products. The authors conclude that AI "expands markets rather than merely shifting sales from non-adopters to adopters" [primary] (same URL)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Adoption is self-selected. Matched DiD and an IV help, but sellers who choose to adopt may also be the ones about to grow, and the abstract does not say what the instrument is. One platform, one country, small firms only. A 51.6% sales increase is enormous compared with typical productivity estimates. The market-expansion claim is the most important one for welfare and also the hardest to identify.
- Mia hook: "If you were a Dutch accounting-software vendor, this paper reads like a product spec: put the AI where the small firm already works. Which Dutch platform (Exact, Bol, Moneybird) is best placed to run this experiment?"

### 4. TaskNet: an LLM interviewer builds better job-task lists than O*NET
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is AI as a research instrument. LLM-led qualitative interviews, standardised and aggregated at scale, beat the official occupational database on coverage. Every AI-exposure estimate he reads rests on O*NET-style task lists, so the input data itself can shift the results.
- Facts:
  - NBER Working Paper 35987 (November 2026) by Jennifer Wang, Andreas Haupt, Isaac Robinson, Phil Lewis, Erik Brynjolfsson and Diyi Yang introduces the Stanford Task Network (TaskNet) [primary] (https://www.nber.org/papers/w35987)
  - Method: a mixed-initiative system conducts interviews with workers, standardises the reports into individual task lists, and aggregates those into occupational task inventories [primary] (same URL)
  - Applied to six occupations: 132 workers interviewed, 2,726 reports, 202 task statements, all accepted by O*NET Center analysts. Only about 65% of the write-in task suggestions collected through O*NET's current process are accepted [primary] (same URL)
  - Validation: 318 incumbents gave 7,923 ratings of relevance and importance. Against O*NET 31.0, TaskNet covers 75% of the 118 published tasks, adds 79 tasks with no O*NET counterpart and refines 58. It covers 89% of tasks reported by held-out workers versus 79% for O*NET, and 72.5% versus 70.2% of activities in employer job postings [primary] (same URL)
  - Downstream, it shifts AI-exposure estimates in both directions, raising them for Software Developers and Lawyers [primary] (same URL)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Six occupations and 132 workers is a pilot, not a taxonomy. The job-postings advantage is small (72.5 vs 70.2). Interviewees who sign up for an AI interview may differ from typical workers, and the LLM interviewer can steer which tasks get mentioned. "Accepted by O*NET analysts" measures acceptability, not truth.
- Mia hook: "I've used an LLM to run user interviews. It's great at follow-up questions, but it's also great at leading the witness. How would you test whether the interviewer is inventing tasks the worker never really does?"

## Deep dive: No second run. How to close self-improvement loops when the expensive experiment cannot be repeated
- Why this topic, why now: The paper "No Second Run: Does RSI Stop at Frontier Pre-training?" (arXiv 2611.10719, submitted 7 November 2026, 59 authors, GAIR-NLP) is one of the most concrete published accounts of agents doing real work inside a project whose central experiment, a months-long pre-training run, happens only once. The model itself is a side story. For Lucas the transferable part is the loop design: how to split an unrepeatable, slow-feedback project into small loops that each have a cheap, checkable signal, where humans approve, and why the obvious metric can lie. The same problem shows up in his own agent pipelines, and in empirical economics, where history happens only once. (Past deep dives covered self-improving harnesses and manager agents that send work back. This one is about loop design when you cannot rerun.)
- Outline:
  1. **What RSI needs and why pre-training breaks it.** The authors define recursive self-improvement operationally as propose → run → measure → update, where a system "turns its own experience and feedback into persistent changes that improve its capability". Post-training RSI relies on three conditions: cheap, repeatable trials (GPU-hours or less), a reliable signal aligned with the true goal, and an existing base model to start from. Pre-training breaks all three. A run takes "hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours over months" and cannot be repeated. The real goal, post-training headroom, "cannot be read off any single score". And what has to improve is the whole production system, not one model. Mechanism: no outer loop can close, so you have to find inner loops that can.
  2. **Decompose into loops with their own verifiers.** The paper describes several loops, each with its own signal and pace. Infra and data debugging loops use "does the anomaly disappear?" as the verifier and iterate in days. Classifier development uses task-specific accuracy/F1 and iterates in weeks. Site discovery uses verified yield per discovery round. Crawl coverage uses retrieved pages divided by estimated pages. One slow "headroom/recipe" loop iterates over months between pilots and the main run. Mechanism: each small loop has a cheap, repeatable check, so agents can iterate inside it. Only the outer loop stays slow and human-steered.
  3. **Context infrastructure turns negative results into memory.** Agents work against a shared record that "records not only what was tried but what failed and why, so that negative results become persistent knowledge". It holds per-layer loss, gradient norms, expert load-balance statistics, more than 130 infra code versions, 106 hyperparameter fields, data-mixture edits and checkpoint evaluations. Example: aggregate load-balance metrics looked fine, but layers 15 and 18 showed "large, persistent MaxVio violations". Agents correlated the symptom with the code history and traced it to a change in the load-balance penalty and its interaction with optimizer state. The fixes: coefficient 10⁻² → 10⁻⁴, learning rate 2.7×10⁻³ → 5×10⁻⁴, the router optimizer switched from Muon to AdamW, and global-batch instead of per-micro-batch balancing. The authors report about one month to diagnose all five cross-stack issues, versus work that "can take months" without that context. Mechanism: diagnosis becomes a search over a versioned, joined record of changes and symptoms. That is something agents are good at, as long as the record exists.
  4. **Data debugging: from strange outputs back to pipeline stages.** Three anomalies were traced across 6 pipelines, more than 30 processing stages and 1,053 data subsets. (a) The model emitted API keys and repeated proxy-config blocks, traced to poorly cleaned config files in the code corpus. (b) It jumped to a fixed "Does this sentence make sense?" QA template, traced to unfiltered synthetic data in an open-source QA subset. (c) It generated endless identical division problems after a "**New Example:**" marker, traced to low prompt diversity in a synthetic-data pipeline. The human role is explicitly limited to two decisions: "investigation prioritization and fix approval." Mechanism: humans sit at the decision points, not in the search.
  5. **The proxy that lies: false stagnation and ranking disagreement.** Base-model scores plateaued (around 61 between 4.1T and 7T tokens) while scores after a fixed small SFT kept rising (65.34 → 66.83). Later, base scores sat around 64–66 while post-SFT scores rose from 70.54 to 72.93. Checkpoint rankings by base score and by post-SFT score correlate with Spearman ρ = 0.796 in one stage and only 0.438 in later stages. So the team measured headroom with a "tiny SFT" (6.33B tokens: about 2B each of math, science and code, plus 0.331B of instruction following, no RL) and a "learning aptitude" slope: the score gain per tenfold increase in post-training data, from a log-linear fit. Mechanism: when the cheap metric and the true goal diverge, you insert a cheap simulation of the downstream step and measure that instead.
  6. **What it bought, and the limits.** Mean score over 22 benchmarks at a matched 1T-token budget: initial pilot 47.78, revised pilot 51.96, main run 56.83. At 3T tokens: revised pilot 56.48, main run 59.94. A separate fix, sorting packed sequences by attention cost and assigning packs to the least-loaded rank, gave a 2.26× throughput gain (1,126 → 2,543 TGS) on 256-GPU, 256K-context runs. The result is 801-30B-A3B-Base, trained on 11.8T tokens in three months. It beats Qwen3-30B-A3B and Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B as a base model, trails only Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and keeps that position under identical SFT. Limits the authors state: SFT was not repeated across random seeds, so small checkpoint differences need caution. Evaluation so far focuses on agentic tasks. Several fixes changed together, so "the comparison does not isolate the contribution of any single adjustment". Once a question couples architecture, recipe, infrastructure and data, "the proxy study needed to settle it becomes as expensive as the run it was meant to de-risk." Only the base model and the SFT data are released; full post-training is left for a later release.
- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Title, submission date (7 November 2026), RSI framing, three conditions, 801-30B-A3B trained on 11.8T tokens in three months by a newly formed team [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10719)
  - Loop decomposition, context infrastructure, load-balance diagnosis and fixes, human roles ("investigation prioritization and fix approval"), the three data anomalies, 130+ code versions, 106 hyperparameter fields, 1,053 subsets, 30+ stages [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10719)
  - False stagnation numbers (61 flat vs 65.34 → 66.83; 64–66 vs 70.54 → 72.93), Spearman ρ 0.796 / 0.438, tiny-SFT composition, learning-aptitude definition [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10719)
  - Pilot vs main run means (47.78 / 51.96 / 56.83 at 1T; 56.48 / 59.94 at 3T), 2.26× throughput (1,126 → 2,543 TGS) [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10719)
  - Agentic data funnel: 260K raw PRs → 220K executable tasks → about 2M trajectories across 9 harnesses → 1.1M verified trajectories, over 100B tokens [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10719)
  - The abstract claims that with lightweight SFT alone, the model "achieves the highest average on four agentic benchmarks against fully post-trained models, including Qwen3.6-35B-A3B" [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10719)
  - Release: base model and lightweight SFT data, via GitHub (https://github.com/GAIR-NLP/801-30B) and Hugging Face dataset (https://huggingface.co/datasets/SII-GAIR-NLP/801-30B-A3B-tiny-sft-dataset). At the time of the community post, model weights were listed as "Coming Soon" [primary for paper, secondary for the "Coming Soon" status] (https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1xwt3ri/paper_no_second_run_does_rsi_stop_at_frontier/)
  - The paper reports no failed agent diagnoses, and it does not say which agent models were used. Treat agent effectiveness as the authors' self-report [primary, from the absence in the paper] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10719)
- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week: Take one slow or one-shot workflow (a monthly report, a long research pipeline, an agent that runs overnight) and write down its loops explicitly. For each loop, record (1) what changes, (2) the cheap check that says it got better (ideally "the specific failure no longer reproduces"), and (3) who approves the change. Then add a minimal "context log": an append-only file or SQLite table per run with config/prompt version, inputs, metrics and a one-line "what failed and why". Point a Claude skill at it so diagnosis becomes a search rather than a memory exercise. Finally, check whether the metric he watches is the real goal or a proxy that can plateau while the real outcome improves. If it could be a proxy, add a small "tiny-SFT"-style downstream test, for example a fixed set of real questions answered end-to-end.
