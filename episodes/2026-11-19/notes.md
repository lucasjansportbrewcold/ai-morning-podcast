### 1. Gemini 4 Neon: Google's new mid-tier model costs more per token than its flagship (for now)
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is a new option for agent workloads at Opus/Fable-class quality. Google claims it is 32% cheaper per task than Carbon. The pricing ladder is confusing, though, so you can't pick a Gemini model by its per-token list price. It continues the point from 16 November that price per task is what matters.
- Facts:
  - Google describes Neon as "frontier-level intelligence with fast end-to-end task completion" for software engineering, knowledge work and science. In its lineup, Carbon handles the hardest, longest-horizon projects, Neon balances depth and efficiency for "complex, high-frequency workflows", and Hydrogen does fast everyday execution [primary] (https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)
  - Price: $3 per million input tokens ($0.15 cached) and $15 per million output tokens. It has a 1M-token context window and four configurable thinking levels [primary] (https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)
  - Google says that at the default "high" setting Neon costs 32% less per task than Carbon [primary] (https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)
  - Scores Google reports: Terminal-Bench 4.1 65.2%, SEC-bench Pro 90.5%, SWE-sweep 39.0%, Agent Arena 1336 (#2), #2 on the Vals Index. On agentic safety: 0.09% misaligned-action rate on Google's agentic safety suite and 0.0% indirect prompt-injection success on Gray Swan's benchmark [primary] (https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)
  - Sources disagree on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Google reports 61 (#3). Latent Space's summary of Artificial Analysis says Neon at high effort "matched Carbon's 60" at $3.74 per task [primary for Google's figure; secondary for the Latent Space figure] (https://latent.space/p/ainews-gemini-4-neon-gdm-s-opus-sol-contender/)
  - Vals: 70.9% on the Vals Index, about 0.4 points behind Fable 5.2 at less than half the cost per task. On Vals' Code Migration task Neon used 160 turns on average (Carbon 234, Hydrogen 574) but scored lower than both [secondary] (https://latent.space/p/ainews-gemini-4-neon-gdm-s-opus-sol-contender/)
  - Pricing confusion: Simon Willison notes that Carbon is half price ($2/$10) until 1 December, when it doubles to $4/$20. For now that makes Neon the most expensive per token of Carbon, Neon and Hydrogen ($1.50/$7.50). Neon is the fourth Gemini 4 model in about seven weeks, and Argon has been retired [primary for Willison's post] (https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/18/llm-gemini/), [secondary] (https://latent.space/p/ainews-gemini-4-neon-gdm-s-opus-sol-contender/)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Almost every number comes from Google or from leaderboards Google quotes. The Code Migration result is a useful warning: finishing in fewer turns doesn't mean the work got done. "0.0% prompt-injection success" holds on one benchmark only and says nothing about your own toolset. Releases are now days apart (Arena counted 28, then 15, then 6 days between models), so any eval you run has a short shelf life.
- Mia hook: "I ran my RAG eval set over all three Gemini tiers. Neon's answers were as good as Carbon's, but on 1 December the cheapest option flips again. Do you hard-code a model ID or route on cost per solved task?"

### 2. Cheaper legal expertise, yet top law firms raise prices: evidence from US legal services
- Why it matters for Lucas: This is a carefully hedged economics-of-AI paper with real data on a knowledge-work market. Its mechanism, where premium suppliers raise markups when price-sensitive work leaves, carries over to Dutch consultancy, research and advisory firms like Lucas's own.
- Facts:
  - Paper: "AI, Specialization, and the Organization of Knowledge Work: Evidence from Legal Services" by Liu Zhang, Mark Esposito and Isabel Yang, arXiv 2611.23354, submitted 17 November 2026 [primary] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23354)
  - Design: difference-in-differences across 89 federal districts. Non-prisoner civil cases are the treated group and prisoner petitions the comparison group, with district-by-group and district-by-year fixed effects, FY2010–2025 (FY2016 excluded) [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Result: self-representation rose by +0.3 pp in FY2023 (SE 0.7), +1.5 pp in FY2024 (SE 0.7) and +6.55 pp in FY2025 (SE 0.89). After removing the pre-2022 linear trend, the FY2025 effect is +5.7 pp [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Firms don't bring legal work in-house: the in-house share of private-sector lawyers (ACS microdata) went from 22.8% (2010) to 28.7% (2019) to 29.0% (2024), so most of the growth predates generative AI. Sector-level AI adoption shows no association with in-house hiring (β = −0.004, p = 0.79) [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Am Law 100 gross revenue rose 13.0% in FY2025 while billable hours in a 50-firm Thomson Reuters sample rose 0.1%. Worked rates rose about 9.3%. Midsize firms saw about 5% hours growth in H2 2025 [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Across 41 firms, reported AI integration is stronger at firms with lower revenue per lawyer (Spearman 0.32, p = 0.04) [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Mechanism: when cheaper AI-enabled challengers improve on "flexible" work, that work leaves the premium firm. Because it is more price-sensitive than the "restricted" work that stays, the remaining demand is less elastic and the optimal markup rises while volume falls. The authors call this "a conditional selection result, not a claim that AI always raises premium prices" [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
  - Capability context: Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark all-pass score rose from about 7% in May to 25% in October 2026, while LegalBench reasoning scores reached 89% [primary] (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23354)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: Only the self-representation result has a real identification strategy. The law-firm findings are descriptive, the two data sources cover different sets of firms, and the authors say much of the reallocation predates generative AI. The comparison group (prisoners) may have less access to AI tools, but rising self-representation doesn't tell us how much paid work was actually displaced. The model is partial equilibrium.
- Mia hook: "If AI takes away the easy, price-sensitive work, should a small research bureau compete on price for the routine work, or charge more for the restricted, high-stakes work?"

### 3. Cameranu: a failed chatbot became a team of single-task agents that now handles a quarter of customer contacts
- Why it matters for Lucas: A Dutch SME case with numbers, showing that narrow agents with one owner and systematic conversation review beat one generalist bot. It's a playbook he could use for Dutch SME automation.
- Facts:
  - Flits launched just before Black Friday a year ago and flopped. It was built for transactional questions (order status, cancellation, returns) but got product questions and unexpected ones, such as store opening hours. Cameranu's AI specialist Andy Luijendijk: "We hadden te weinig focus en commitment. Het lag niet aan de AI-tool zelf." ("We lacked focus and commitment. It wasn't the AI tool itself.") [secondary: Emerce report of a conference talk] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/aiassistent-camernu-doet-kwart-klantcontacten)
  - The fix: one project owner, structured analysis of conversations, and one role per agent. Separate agents handle order status, cancellations and lens-to-camera compatibility [secondary] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/aiassistent-camernu-doet-kwart-klantcontacten)
  - Results: the order-status agent's success rate rose from about 40% to 67% and its response time fell from 6 to 3 seconds. Simple, well-scoped questions resolve at around 90%. Flits now handles 25% of all customer contacts, 60% of them fully autonomously, with a handover to a human available [secondary] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/aiassistent-camernu-doet-kwart-klantcontacten)
  - Tech partner: CM.com. Presented at Emerce AI Live! in Rotterdam on 17 November, which drew 2,231 visitors [secondary] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/aiassistent-camernu-doet-kwart-klantcontacten), [primary for attendance] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/emerce-ai-live-2026-fotos)
  - Next step: email support for Black Friday 2026, with AI drafting replies that a human reviews before sending [secondary] (https://www.emerce.nl/nieuws/aiassistent-camernu-doet-kwart-klantcontacten)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: These are vendor and conference numbers. The report doesn't say how "success" is defined (resolved? no follow-up contact?). 67% success also means a third of order-status chats fail. Splitting by role moves the problem to the router that decides which agent gets a chat.
- Mia hook: "What's the router here, an LLM classifier or buttons? And how do you notice the questions no agent owns, like the opening-hours case?"

### 4. Anthropic prices its IPO at $83; what the S-1 shows about the economics of a frontier lab
- Why it matters for Lucas: Beyond the headline, the S-1 is the first audited look at frontier-lab cost structure (compute split, margins, data spend). That is real data for the economics-of-AI side of his work. Tyler Cowen's argument about debt versus equity is a neat economist's way to read it.
- Facts:
  - Priced at $83.00 per share. 980,000,000 shares are offered, 956,351,354 of them by Anthropic. Ticker ANT on the Nasdaq Global Select Market, trading from 19 November 2026, closing expected 20 November. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead [primary] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering)
  - Anthropic's own shares at $83 come to about $79 billion in gross proceeds (our arithmetic: 956,351,354 × $83) [primary for the inputs] (https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering)
  - Commentary on the S-1 (Latent Space with Tanay Jaipuria): about 70% of compute goes to training and research and 30% to inference. They report 85% gross margins on paid inference and roughly $132bn of the ~$140bn raised still on the balance sheet. October run rate was given as $80–81bn, with revenue about 80% consumption and 20% subscriptions. About 80% of usage comes from models released in the last six months [secondary] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wX8nDtGIfYA)
  - Cowen (who discloses he sits on Anthropic's advisory board) reads the IPO as a bearish signal on near-term "extreme superintelligence". If the returns on compute were that high, it would be rational to borrow (he cites roughly 10–11% debt cost) rather than share the upside through equity [primary] (https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/11/the-anthropic-ipo.html)
- Skeptic angle / open questions: The S-1 numbers above come from a podcast, not our own reading of the filing. Run-rate definitions vary. Cowen's argument assumes Anthropic could borrow at scale and ignores liquidity for employees and investors. The "80% of usage from models under six months old" figure shows how fast customers would switch if Anthropic fell behind, which is the same churn risk any agent builder faces.
- Mia hook: "Only about 4% of margin goes to serving free users, so the business is enterprise API. Does that make Claude more or less likely to keep investing in agent tooling like skills and hooks?"

## Deep dive: Front-load the human. Grill, spec and ticket so the agent can run AFK

- Why this topic, why now: Lucas wants autonomous agents while his own role stays small. Matt Pocock (whose skills repo shows about 327.6k GitHub stars) just published a full real-world run: one feature, ~30 sessions, a 21,000-line PR. In it, human effort sits almost entirely at the start (alignment) and the end (review that changes the system), and the middle runs unattended. This complements earlier deep dives on verification (9 Oct), monitors (24 Oct) and durable agents (12 Oct). Those were about controlling the agent while it runs. This one is about shrinking the decisions it has to make before it starts.

- Outline:
  1. **The core claim: decision space is the risk.** Pocock: "as soon as you ask an agent to make a decision, it might make a bad decision. So, the more decisions you give it… the more likely it is to choose a bad one." The workflow goes into implementation with as few open decisions as possible. The mechanism: each unresolved design choice is a point where the agent can go wrong, and fixing those choices in a cheap conversation costs less than reviewing many wrong implementations later. Human effort moves to before the code exists.
  2. **Grilling builds a shared vocabulary first.** The `grill-with-docs` skill interviews the human, and the human does the steering. Along the way it updates a glossary (560 lines in this repo) and writes architecture decision records (ADRs) when a decision is non-obvious, involves a real trade-off and is hard to reverse. The glossary terms later become database tables and module names. In the video the agent proposed "thumbnail drawing"; Pocock rejected it and the agent ran a "scenario test", trying candidate terms in realistic sentences. They settled on "thumbnail diagram", with an ADR explaining why it is not a "diagram" in the system's sense. ADRs serve as Chesterton's fences: a written reason for every odd constraint, so future agent runs don't rip it out.
  3. **Prototypes settle questions about how a design feels.** Questions like "how does it feel to use?" can't be answered in conversation. `prototype` builds throwaway variants (three UI variants here) while grilling continues. Pocock rejected two; seeing what you dislike tells the agent a lot. A bug spotted in the prototype (a white bar at the edge) was traced to Chrome rendering at 125% Windows scaling. The prototype then becomes the spec's reference implementation for the UI.
  4. **Spec and tickets turn alignment into a task graph.** `to-spec` turns the conversation into a "destination document", here a GitHub issue with 47 user stories and an out-of-scope list. `to-tickets` cuts it into 9 "tracer-bullet" tickets: vertical slices through schema, API, UI and tests, each sized to fit one fresh context window. Each has acceptance criteria and explicit "blocked by" edges, with refactoring work ordered first. Ticket text avoids file paths (they go out of date) unless a prototype has pinned down a key type or state machine.
  5. **AFK execution uses fresh contexts and parallel workers.** `implement-spec` treats tickets as a graph. Whenever a ticket finishes, it recomputes the "ready frontier" (tickets whose blockers are done) and starts new subagents, each in its own worktree with a fresh context. Agents pass pointers (spec link, ticket, commits) instead of copying context. A separate merger subagent integrates the work, and `code-review` runs two parallel reviewers that don't see each other's output. One checks repo standards plus Fowler code smells; the other checks faithfulness to the spec. Findings are labelled hard violations or judgement calls, and the two axes are deliberately not ranked against each other. Pocock compacts the grilling session before starting, which he calls "almost free" once the conversation has become a document. He also notes a subagent reusing a 260k-token context was "punching pretty deep into its dumb zone": work quality degrades as context grows, so fresh contexts per ticket are a quality choice, not just a cost choice.
  6. **Review changes the system, not just the PR.** The 21k-line PR is split into stacked diffs, with "two-way doors" (easy to revert) first and the "one-way door" (a schema migration, then a feature-flag flip) last. Only the migration PR got mandatory review. Review comments became rules: a lint rule banning TypeScript `as` outside shared utilities, a ban on `useSearchParams` with magic strings to be tightened over time, and coding-standard limits on comments. `retro` reads the session transcripts and the 12 review comments and proposes ordered improvements to the agent's environment. The human's role becomes maintaining the environment that produces the code.

- Facts and examples with sources:
  - Skill descriptions: grill-with-docs builds the domain model while updating the glossary and ADRs inline. to-tickets produces "tracer-bullet tickets declaring blocking edges". implement-spec builds a spec on one integration branch "managing tickets as a task graph with concurrent subagents across the ready frontier". code-review is a "two-axis review" (Standards vs Spec). retro recommends environment improvements "ordered by severity". Install with `claude plugin install mattpocock-skills@claude-plugins-official`, then `/setup-matt-pocock-skills` per repo [primary] (https://github.com/mattpocock/skills)
  - implement-spec mechanics: a fresh context and its own worktree per implementer; communication via "context pointers"; the TDD skill per ticket; a fresh merger subagent; code review after all tickets, with fixes in one extra subagent pass; worktrees removed at the end [primary] (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattpocock/skills/main/skills/engineering/implement-spec/SKILL.md)
  - Tracer-bullet tickets: a "narrow but COMPLETE path through every layer". Sized to a single context window, refactoring first ("make the change easy, then make the easy change"), expand-contract for wide refactors, no file paths by default [primary] (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattpocock/skills/main/skills/engineering/to-tickets/SKILL.md)
  - Code review: Standards and Spec sub-agents run in parallel with fresh context. Each reports under 400 words. Repo standards override the baseline smells, and rules already enforced by tooling are skipped [primary] (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattpocock/skills/main/skills/engineering/code-review/SKILL.md)
  - Run numbers: 21,000-line PR, about 30 sessions in the retro, 12 review comments, 47 user stories, 9 sub-issues [primary] (https://www.aihero.dev/watch-me-ship-a-21-000-line-feature-with-mattpocock-skills~ez95n)
  - Setup in the video: Claude Code with Opus 5.5 at medium effort, dictation via Whisper Flow, a 560-line glossary, a stack of 7 PRs with automerge on CI for the low-risk ones [primary] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jVBlkOF8sHk)
  - Pocock credits the "smart zone / dumb zone" idea to Dex Horthy and now frames it as cost and quality optimisation rather than mainly hallucination risk [primary] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jVBlkOF8sHk)

- Practical takeaway Lucas could apply this week: Pick one research-automation task he wants to hand off, say a data-extraction pipeline from reports. Before any agent writes code, run a 20-minute "grilling" session that has to produce (a) a glossary of the key terms (what counts as a "series", a "revision", a "source table"), (b) one ADR for the least obvious choice, and (c) 3–6 vertical tickets, each with acceptance criteria and blocked-by edges. Then let the agent run each ticket in a fresh context. Finally, turn every review comment he makes into a rule (a lint, a test or a line in CLAUDE.md) instead of a one-off fix. Measure how many decisions the agent still had to escalate.
