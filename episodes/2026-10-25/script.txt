GEORGE: That's the crux. Now, why hasn't this been answered already? Measuring it needs three things at once. Sustained use, not a one-afternoon lab session. A measure of skill that's separate from AI-assisted output. And experimental control over who gets access.
MIA: And the second one is the killer, right? If you only ever look at work done with the tool, you can't tell whether the person got better or the tool did.
GEORGE: Precisely. The authors say no previous study had all three in a high-stakes professional setting. That's the gap they're going after.
MIA: Okay, walk me through the design, because I suspect that's where the cleverness is.
GEORGE: It is. A hundred and thirty-three patent lawyers at eleven US intellectual property firms, all of which have drafting relationships with Google. Randomised two to one within firm, stratified by experience.
MIA: Two to one, so more treated than control. Why?
GEORGE: It was a compromise. The researchers wanted an even split, the firms wanted to give more of their lawyers access sooner. So you end up with ninety in treatment and forty-three in control.
MIA: That's the firms' business interest leaking into the design. Not fatal, but it costs you precision on the control side.
GEORGE: Fair. A bit more context: there were twenty-four firms with regular, non-exclusive business with Google, and eleven agreed to take part. Firms put forward a hundred and fifty-six lawyers, and a hundred and thirty-three of them, eighty-five percent, were randomised. Participants were paid their contractual hourly rate for the time spent on the tasks.
MIA: Which matters, because otherwise you'd get rushed work. What was the tool?
GEORGE: An unreleased Google Labs writing assistant. Prompt-based text modification, feedback from several AI "editorial personas", and rule-based checks for internal inconsistencies. A redlining feature was added in July twenty twenty-five. It never shipped as a standalone product; its features were folded into other Google AI products.
MIA: And control got what, a typewriter?
GEORGE: Standard legal tools, but no generative AI writing tools. Both arms got basic AI training, and treatment also got tool-specific training, including a patent-specific prompt library and material on common pitfalls.
MIA: Now the tasks.
GEORGE: Three of them. At ten days, an AI-assisted drafting task: five to seven dependent claims plus a detailed description, at most a thousand words, at most two hours. At ninety days, a harder drafting task, up to two thousand words. And also at ninety days, the important one: a redlining task done without AI. They're handed an AI-generated patent draft and have to mark it up.
MIA: Oh, that's nice. Redlining an AI draft is basically the acceptor job. You're not producing text, you're judging it.
GEORGE: That's exactly why they picked it as the skill measure. It draws on judgment rather than text production. And the core trick is that the same people are measured once with the tool and once without it.
MIA: And I assume they made sure the model hadn't seen the inventions?
GEORGE: Invention details came from a hold-out set not used to train the model. Scoring was done by blinded raters from an independent IP firm, two raters per task, drawn from a pool of six. The rubric was developed with Google's in-house counsel and covers five dimensions: enforceability, technical accuracy, strategic ambiguity, completeness and clarity.
MIA: Strategic ambiguity is such a lawyer thing. You want the claim vague in exactly the right places.

MIA: Okay. Result one. What happened to drafting with the tool?

MIA: So the classic leveling pattern. Same shape as Noy and Zhang, Brynjolfsson and colleagues, and the rest of that literature.

MIA: Where does the gain come from, though? Better top work or less bad work?
GEORGE: Fewer poor drafts, not more excellent ones. The share of bottom-quintile scores fell by point one nine at ten days. At ninety days it fell by point one one, though that one isn't significant. And the variance compressed significantly on the ninety-day task.
MIA: That fits everything I see. These tools raise the floor. They stop you writing something embarrassing; they don't make you brilliant.

MIA: Noted. But if you stopped there, the headline would be "AI makes juniors as good as seniors".

MIA: So the ordering flips. With the tool, juniors gain most. Without it, juniors gain nothing and seniors gain a lot.

MIA: What about the controls? Is there just a natural senior advantage at redlining?
GEORGE: That's the striking bit. Among controls, juniors and seniors performed comparably. The point estimate even slightly favoured juniors. So three months of AI drafting opened a junior–senior gap in unassisted judgment that doesn't exist among people who didn't get the tool.
MIA: Huh. So in this sample, the AI created the expertise gap. That's quite a sentence.

MIA: So the middle hollowed out. Some juniors got better, some got worse, and they average to nothing.
GEORGE: More dispersion among juniors. The seniors went the other way: less dispersed, more good scores, fewer poor ones. Neither group saw a significant change in top-quintile scores.
MIA: How do the authors put it?
GEORGE: AI was "a springboard for some juniors and a cushion for others". And their reading is that foundational expertise may be needed to turn AI-assisted practice into lasting skill.
